## The Original 33 Expelled (1868)

Sources:
1. "Original 33." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/original-33/.
2. "'I Claim the Rights of a Man': The Story of Georgia's Original 33 Black Legislators." WABE, https://www.wabe.org/the-story-of-georgias-original-33-black-legislators/.
3. "'Am I a Man?': The Fiery 1868 Speech by an Expelled Black Legislator in Georgia." *Facing South*, Institute for Southern Studies, Apr. 2023, https://www.facingsouth.org/2023/04/1868-speech-expelled-black-georgia-legislator-henry-mcneal-turner.
4. "They Were Expelled from the Georgia Legislature. Now They're Honored." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, Feb. 2026, https://images.ajc.com/politics/2026/02/they-were-expelled-from-the-georgia-legislature-now-theyre-honored.
5. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 134–36.

South-View Residents Involved
* Bishop Henry McNeal Turner – Elected to the legislature in 1868, expelled with the other Black members, and seated again in 1870 (pg. 135). He rests at South-View (pg. 134).

Historical Notes
* In 1868, 33 Black men were elected to the Georgia General Assembly, 30 to the House and 3 to the Senate. (Source 4)
* They began their terms in the new capital, Atlanta, on July 4, 1868. (Source 1)
* In September 1868, white legislators voted to expel the Black members. (Sources 1, 2)
* On September 3, 1868, Turner answered with the speech remembered as "I Claim the Rights of a Man." WABE reports it was the first time a Black man officially spoke on the assembly floor. (Sources 2, 3)
* The expelled members retook their seats in 1870. (pg. 135; Source 1)
* Turner had been the first of fourteen Black chaplains in the Union Army and helped organize Georgia's Republican Party. (pg. 135)

Narrative for website

In 1868, three years after the Civil War, Georgia voters elected thirty-three Black men to the state legislature, thirty to the House and three to the Senate ("They Were Expelled"). They took their seats in Atlanta that July ("Original 33"). Two months later, white legislators voted to expel them because of their race ("Original 33"; "I Claim the Rights of a Man"). On September 3, Rev. Henry McNeal Turner, the first Black chaplain in the Union Army and one of the expelled men, rose to answer them: "Am I a man? If I am such, I claim the rights of a man" ("Am I a Man?"; pg. 135). The Original 33, as they came to be called, retook their seats in 1870 (pg. 135). Turner later became a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and when he died in 1915, thousands followed his funeral procession to South-View, where he rests (pg. 134).

## Oakland's Slave Square Dug Up (1877)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 1, 7–10.
2. "Telling the Story of Slave Square." Historic Oakland Foundation, https://www.oaklandcemetery.com/blog/telling-the-story-of-slave-square.
3. "Illuminating the History of Slave Square." Historic Oakland Foundation, https://www.oaklandcemetery.com/blog/illuminating-the-history-of-slave-square.

South-View Residents Involved
* Albert Watts – A South-View founder. His mother, Emily Watts, was buried at Oakland in 1876 (pg. 8).
* The six founders of South-View – Chartered their own cemetery in 1886 (pg. 1).

Historical Notes
* In 1852, the city set apart a section of Oakland Cemetery for "burying the black population." Enslaved and free Black people were buried there, and it came to be called Slave Square. (pg. 8)
* By the end of the Civil War, about 860 people were reportedly buried in Slave Square. (pg. 8)
* In April 1877, the Atlanta City Council voted to remove the bodies from Slave Square, level the ground and sell new lots, which went to white families. (pg. 9; Sources 2, 3)
* The council told the sexton to rebury people whose graves had headboards in individual graves in the "colored pauper grounds." People in unmarked graves were apparently moved without names to a mass grave whose location is unknown. (pg. 9)
* In early 1878, Black Atlantans sent the city council two petitions, one for more burial space at Oakland and one for a separate cemetery. No final decision has been found. (pg. 9–10)

Narrative for website

When Atlanta opened Oakland Cemetery, the city set apart a corner of it for "burying the black population," and enslaved and free Black Atlantans were laid to rest in the section that came to be called Slave Square (pg. 8). In April 1877, with Oakland's other lots filling up, the Atlanta City Council voted to dig up Slave Square, level the ground and sell the space to white families ("Telling the Story of Slave Square"; pg. 9). People whose graves had headboards were reburied in the "colored pauper grounds," but those in unmarked graves were apparently moved without their names to a mass grave whose location is still unknown (pg. 9). Early the next year, Black Atlantans petitioned the city twice, once for more burial space and once for a cemetery of their own (pg. 9–10). The city gave no answer that has been found. Eight years later, Albert Watts, whose mother had been buried at Oakland in 1876, and five other formerly enslaved men chartered South-View (pg. 1, 8).

## Grady's Training School for Black Nurses (1914)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 6, 171.
2. "Timeline of Major Events." *Grady Memorial Hospital School of Nursing* exhibit, Georgia State University Library, https://exhibits.library.gsu.edu/grady-exhibit/timeline-of-major-events/.
3. "National Conclave of Grady Graduate Nurses Collection." Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History, finding aid, https://aspace-aafa.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/125.
4. "Ludie Clay Andrews." Georgia Women of Achievement, https://www.georgiawomen.org/ludie-clay-andrews.

South-View Residents Involved
* Ludie Clay Andrews – Organized the school in 1914 and led it until 1922; Georgia's first Black registered nurse (pg. 171). She rests at South-View (pg. 6).

Historical Notes
* Grady Memorial Hospital opened its nursing school in 1898, but Black women were not offered training until 1914, when Ludie Clay Andrews organized the Grady Municipal Training School for Colored Nurses. (pg. 171; Source 2)
* The school received its charter from the state in 1917. (Source 2)
* Students took classes at Spelman and trained in Grady's wards for Black patients. (Source 2)
* While she built the school, Andrews fought the Georgia Board of Nurse Examiners for ten years, at her own expense, for Black nurses' right to take the same licensing exam as white nurses. She won in 1920, the year the school's first class graduated, and became Georgia's first Black registered nurse. (Sources 3, 4)
* From 1922, the white nursing officials at Grady directed the school, and in 1965 the two schools were combined. (pg. 171)

Narrative for website

Grady Memorial Hospital opened a nursing school in 1898, but it offered no training to Black women until 1914, when nurse Ludie Clay Andrews organized the Grady Municipal Training School for Colored Nurses (pg. 171; "Timeline of Major Events"). Its students took classes at Spelman and trained in Grady's wards for Black patients, and the school received its state charter in 1917 ("Timeline of Major Events"). Georgia would not let Black nurses sit for the licensing exam that white nurses took, so while she built the school, Andrews fought the state nursing board for ten years at her own expense ("National Conclave of Grady Graduate Nurses Collection"). She won in 1920, the year the school's first class graduated, and became Georgia's first Black registered nurse ("National Conclave"; "Ludie Clay Andrews"). The school ran separately until 1965, when Grady combined it with the school for white nurses (pg. 171). Andrews rests at South-View (pg. 6).

---

* **Ludie Clay Andrews (d. 1969)** was born in Milledgeville, Georgia, and graduated from the nurse training program at Spelman's MacVicar Hospital in 1906 (pg. 171; "Ludie Clay Andrews"). That same year she became superintendent of Atlanta's Lula Grove Training School for Colored Nurses (pg. 171). In 1914 she organized Grady Hospital's first training school for Black nurses and led it until 1922 (pg. 171). She fought for ten years for Black nurses' right to take Georgia's licensing exam, and in 1920 she became the state's first Black registered nurse ("National Conclave"; "Ludie Clay Andrews"). From 1928 to 1948 she was superintendent of MacVicar Hospital (pg. 171). She was an original board member of the Atlanta Urban League and a charter member of the Neighborhood Union, which she led as its second president from 1937 to 1942 (pg. 171). Nurses in Georgia remember her as "the Dean of Black Nurses," and in 2018 she was named a Georgia Woman of Achievement ("Ludie Clay Andrews"). She rests at South-View (pg. 6).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 6, 171; "Ludie Clay Andrews," Georgia Women of Achievement; "National Conclave of Grady Graduate Nurses Collection," AARL finding aid.

## Atlanta Branch of the NAACP Founded (1917)

Sources:
1. "National Association for the Advancement of Colored People Atlanta Branch Records." Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History, finding aid, https://aspace-aafa.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/9.
2. "Walter White." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/walter-white-1893-1955.
3. "White, Walter F. (1893–1955)." BlackPast, https://www.blackpast.org/aah/white-walter-f-1893-1955.
4. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 120, 185, 212, 253–54.

South-View Residents Involved
* Rev. Adam Daniel Williams – Helped organize the branch in 1917 and later served as its president (pg. 254).
* George White and his wife – Walter White's parents rest at South-View; his ashes were scattered elsewhere (pg. 120).
* Virgil W. Hodges – Chaired the branch's administrative board in the 1940s (pg. 212).
* Rev. Samuel W. Williams and John H. Calhoun Jr. – Later presidents of the branch (pg. 185, 254).

Historical Notes
* The Atlanta branch was chartered in 1917. Its early leaders included James Weldon Johnson, Harry Pace, Dr. Charles Johnson, Dr. Louis Wright and Walter White. (Source 1)
* Rev. A.D. Williams of Ebenezer Baptist Church helped organize the branch and later served as its president. (pg. 254)
* With White as its secretary, the branch stopped the school board from eliminating the seventh grade in Atlanta's Black public schools. The board had planned the cut to help pay for a new high school for white students. (Sources 2, 3)
* White moved to the national office in New York and later led the NAACP. (Sources 2, 3)
* Walter White traced his activism to the night in 1906 when he and his father stood guard at their home during the massacre. (pg. 120)

Narrative for website

The Atlanta branch of the NAACP was chartered in 1917, eleven years after the massacre of 1906 ("NAACP Atlanta Branch Records"). Its organizers included Harry Pace, Dr. Louis Wright, and a young insurance clerk named Walter White, who became its secretary, along with Rev. Adam Daniel Williams of Ebenezer Baptist Church, the grandfather of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. ("NAACP Atlanta Branch Records"; "Walter White"; pg. 254). Its first fight came that year. The school board planned to drop the seventh grade from the city's Black public schools to help pay for a new high school for white students, and the branch's protest stopped it ("Walter White"; "White, Walter F."). White went on to lead the national NAACP, while Williams later served as the Atlanta branch's president (pg. 254; "Walter White"). Several South-View residents carried the branch's work forward, among them Virgil W. Hodges and later presidents Rev. Samuel W. Williams and John H. Calhoun Jr., and Walter White's parents also rest at South-View (pg. 120, 185, 212, 254).

---

* **Adam Daniel Williams (d. 1931)** became pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church in 1894, succeeding its first pastor, John Andrew Parker (pg. 234). In his first year he grew the congregation from a handful of people to more than sixty members, and he built Ebenezer into a center of political and community life as well as worship (pg. 253). He served as president of the Atlanta Baptist Ministers' Union and received a doctor of divinity degree from Morehouse College (pg. 253–54). In 1917 he helped organize the Atlanta branch of the NAACP, and he later served as its president (pg. 254). He married Jennie Celeste Parks (d. 1941), who took part in nearly every area of Ebenezer's life and led its missionary society for many years (pg. 254). Their daughter, Alberta Williams King, was the mother of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. (pg. 254). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 234, 253–54.

## Citizens Trust Bank Opens on Auburn Avenue (1921)

Sources:
1. "Citizens Trust Bank." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/business-economy/citizens-trust-bank.
2. "Citizens Trust Bank." Georgia Historical Society, historical marker, 2025, https://www.georgiahistory.com/?p=67812.
3. "Funderburg, I. Owen 1924–2002." *Contemporary Black Biography*, via Encyclopedia.com, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/funderburg-i-owen-1924-2002.
4. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 4, 32–34, 148, 229, 258.
5. "Our Legacy." Citizens Trust Bank, https://ctbconnect.com/history/.

South-View Residents Involved
* Clayton R. Yates – Chairman of the bank's board; president of the South-View Cemetery Association (pg. 33, 258).
* Lorimer D. Milton – President of the bank; South-View board member (pg. 33, 229).
* Jesse B. Blayton Sr. – Business partner of Milton and Yates (pg. 229).

Historical Notes
* On August 16, 1921, Heman E. Perry and four other Black businessmen opened Citizens Trust Bank on Auburn Avenue. (Sources 1, 2)
* At the time, Black business owners had great difficulty getting loans from mainstream banks. (Source 3)
* The bank reorganized in 1927, with Clayton R. Yates as acting chairman and Lorimer D. Milton as cashier-treasurer. Milton later became president. (Source 1)
* In 1934 Citizens Trust became the first Black-owned bank insured by the FDIC, and in 1947 the first Black-owned bank to join the Federal Reserve System. (Sources 2, 5; Source 1 gives 1948 for the Federal Reserve; Henderson gives 1936, pg. 32)
* Yates and Milton were also partners in the Yates and Milton drugstores. In the 1960s, student organizers met at the Auburn Avenue store for secret planning sessions. (pg. 148)
* By 1961, Citizens Trust was trustee of South-View's perpetual care fund, and Yates was president of the South-View Cemetery Association. (pg. 32–33)

Narrative for website

On August 16, 1921, businessman Heman E. Perry and four partners opened Citizens Trust Bank on Auburn Avenue, at a time when Black business owners had great difficulty getting loans from white-owned banks ("Citizens Trust Bank"; "Funderburg, I. Owen"). The bank reorganized in 1927 with Clayton R. Yates as chairman and Lorimer D. Milton as cashier, and Milton later became its president ("Citizens Trust Bank"). In 1934 it became the first Black-owned bank insured by the FDIC, and in the late 1940s the first to join the Federal Reserve System ("Citizens Trust Bank" marker; "Citizens Trust Bank," NGE). Yates and Milton were also partners in the Yates and Milton drugstores, and in the 1960s student organizers of the civil rights movement met at their Auburn Avenue store to plan in secret (pg. 148). Both men served on South-View's board, Yates as its president, and by 1961 Citizens Trust held South-View's perpetual care fund (pg. 32–33, 258). Yates and Milton rest at South-View.

---

* **Lorimer Douglas Milton (d. 1986)** earned his bachelor's and master's degrees at Brown University (pg. 229). John Hope, the first Black president of Morehouse College and also a Brown graduate, asked him to teach at Morehouse, and Milton kept teaching there while he worked at Citizens Trust Bank (pg. 229). He became the bank's president and was a partner with Clayton Yates in the Yates and Milton drugstore chain (pg. 229). With Yates and Jesse B. Blayton, he started other businesses, including the Top Hat club and Southeastern Fidelity Fire Insurance Company (pg. 229). He chaired the board of trustees of Howard University in Washington, D.C. (pg. 33). He served on South-View's board, as its first vice president in 1976, and he rests at South-View (pg. 34, 229).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 33–34, 229.

* **Clayton Russell Yates Sr. (d. 1977)**, a Morehouse College graduate, was co-owner and chief operating officer of the Yates and Milton drugstore chain and chairman of the board of Citizens Trust Bank (pg. 258). He was the first Black member of the Fulton-DeKalb Hospital Authority and vice president of Southeastern Fire Insurance Company and Mutual Federal Savings and Loan (pg. 258). He was president of the South-View Cemetery Association when the cemetery marked its seventy-fifth anniversary in 1961 (pg. 4, 33). His wife, Mae Maxwell Yates (d. 1980), directed the Carrie Steele-Pitts Home, which in 1950 was still the only full-time child care agency for Black children in Atlanta (pg. 258). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 4, 33, 258.

## The Lynching of Dennis Hubert (1930)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 214.
2. "Killing of Dennis Hubert in Georgia in 1930." Civil Rights and Restorative Justice Project, Northeastern University School of Law, https://crrjarchive.org/incidents/196.
3. "Morehouse College Student Given Degree 95 Years After His Lynching." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, May 2025, https://www.ajc.com/education/morehouse-college-student-given-degree-95-years-after-his-lynching/LA7YEFCXTBF5FDEH4AAGEW52UQ/.
4. "Lynching in America / The Lynching of Dennis Hubert." Historical marker, Fulton County Remembrance Coalition and Equal Justice Initiative, via the Historical Marker Database, https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=227409.
5. "A Black 18-Year-Old College Student Was Lynched on a Playground 95 Years Ago. His Nephew Just Accepted His Posthumous Degree." CNN, via KVIA, 25 May 2025, https://kvia.com/cnn-national/2025/05/25/a-black-18-year-old-college-student-was-lynched-on-a-playground-95-years-ago-his-nephew-just-accepted-his-posthumous-degree/.
6. "Funeral Rites for Charles Dubois Hubert." *Campus Mirror* (Spelman College), 1 Feb. 1944, p. 3, Georgia Historic Newspapers, https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn38019897/1944-02-01/ed-1/seq-3.

South-View Residents Involved
* Dennis Taylor Hubert – Rests at South-View (pg. 214). He has his own card.
* Rev. Gaddus Johnson Hubert and Pearl Parks Hubert – His parents (pg. 214).
* Rev. Charles D. Hubert – His uncle, director of Morehouse's School of Religion and acting president from 1937 to 1940; buried at South-View in 1944 (pg. 214; Source 6).

Historical Notes
* Hubert was eighteen and a divinity student at Morehouse College. (pg. 214; Source 3)
* His father, Rev. G.J. Hubert, was a prominent Baptist minister, and his mother was a principal at Crogman School. (pg. 214; Source 5)
* On June 15, 1930, on the playground of the segregated Crogman School in the Pittsburgh neighborhood, seven white men confronted him. One of their wives said he had insulted her, and one of the men shot and killed him. (pg. 214; Sources 2, 3)
* Two days after the accused men were denied bail, his parents' home was burned to the ground. When a Black Baptist church held a fundraiser to rebuild the home and support the prosecution, a white mob attacked it with tear gas. (Source 5; pg. 214)
* Juries convicted the shooter of voluntary manslaughter and six men as accessories. (pg. 214; Source 2)
* In 2022 the Fulton County Remembrance Coalition and the Equal Justice Initiative placed a marker in his memory. (Source 4)
* In May 2025, Morehouse awarded him a posthumous degree. (Source 3)

Narrative for website

Dennis Taylor Hubert was eighteen, a divinity student at Morehouse College, and the son of a prominent Baptist minister, Rev. G.J. Hubert (pg. 214; "A Black 18-Year-Old College Student"). On June 15, 1930, on the playground of the segregated Crogman School in the Pittsburgh neighborhood, seven white men confronted him over a claim that he had insulted a white woman, and one of them shot and killed him (pg. 214; "Killing of Dennis Hubert"). As Black Atlanta pressed for justice, the Huberts' home was burned to the ground two days after the accused men were denied bail, and a white mob attacked a Black church with tear gas during a fundraiser for the family ("A Black 18-Year-Old College Student"; pg. 214). Juries convicted the shooter of voluntary manslaughter and six other men as accessories (pg. 214; "Killing of Dennis Hubert"). In 2022, a historical marker was placed in his memory, and in 2025 Morehouse College awarded him a posthumous degree ("Lynching of Dennis Hubert" marker; "Morehouse College Student Given Degree"). He rests at South-View (pg. 214).

* **Dennis Taylor Hubert (1911–1930)** was a sophomore in the divinity program at Morehouse College ("Killing of Dennis Hubert"; pg. 214). His father, Rev. G.J. Hubert, was a prominent Baptist minister, and his mother, Pearl Parks Hubert, was a principal at Crogman School in the Pittsburgh neighborhood (pg. 214; "A Black 18-Year-Old College Student"). His uncle, Rev. Charles D. Hubert, directed Morehouse's School of Religion and served as the college's acting president from 1937 to 1940 (pg. 214). When he was killed, Dennis had been working as a driver for John Hope, Morehouse's first Black president ("A Black 18-Year-Old College Student"). On June 15, 1930, at eighteen, he was lynched by a group of white men on the playground of Crogman School (pg. 214; "Morehouse College Student Given Degree"). In 2022, the Fulton County Remembrance Coalition and the Equal Justice Initiative placed a marker in his memory, and in May 2025, Morehouse awarded him a posthumous bachelor's degree ("Lynching of Dennis Hubert" marker; "Morehouse College Student Given Degree"). He rests at South-View (pg. 214).
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 214; "Killing of Dennis Hubert in Georgia in 1930," CRRJ Archive; "A Black 18-Year-Old College Student Was Lynched on a Playground 95 Years Ago," CNN, 2025; "Morehouse College Student Given Degree 95 Years After His Lynching," AJC, 2025; "The Lynching of Dennis Hubert" marker, HMdb.

## The 1946 Voter Registration Drive (1946)

Sources:
1. "Black Suffrage in the Twentieth Century." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/government-politics/black-suffrage-in-the-twentieth-century/.
2. "Clarence A. Bacote." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/clarence-a-bacote-1906-1981/.
3. Smith, Halle. "Atlanta Negro Voters League: Strength in Unity." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/atlanta-negro-voters-league-strength-in-unity/.
4. "Atlanta Negro Voters League." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/government-politics/atlanta-negro-voters-league-anvl/.
5. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 185, 204, 254–55.

South-View Residents Involved
* Grace Towns Hamilton – Executive director of the Atlanta Urban League from 1943 (pg. 204).
* John Wesley Dobbs – Co-founded the Atlanta Negro Voters League in 1949 (Sources 3, 4).
* John H. Calhoun Jr. – Co-founded the Atlanta Negro Voters League (pg. 185).
* Q.V. Williamson Sr. – A leader of the Atlanta Negro Voters League (pg. 255).

Historical Notes
* In October 1945, after Primus King sued, federal judge T. Hoyt Davis ruled Georgia's all-white Democratic primary unconstitutional. (Source 3)
* In 1946, in *King v. Chapman*, the federal appeals court upheld the ruling. (Source 1)
* In 1946, the Atlanta Urban League led a coalition of Black Republicans, Black Democrats and NAACP members that registered 14,368 new Black voters in about two months. (Source 1)
* The Atlanta All-Citizens Registration Committee, chaired by Atlanta University historian Clarence A. Bacote, raised the number of Black registered voters in the city from 6,976 to 21,244 in less than five months. (Source 2)
* By June 1946, Black voters made up 27.2 percent of Atlanta's electorate. (Source 3)
* On July 7, 1949, John Wesley Dobbs and attorney A.T. Walden founded the Atlanta Negro Voters League at the Butler Street YMCA. (Sources 3, 4)

Narrative for website

For decades, Georgia's Democratic Party barred Black citizens from voting in its primary elections ("Black Suffrage in the Twentieth Century"). In October 1945, after Primus King of Columbus sued, a federal judge ruled the white primary unconstitutional, and an appeals court upheld the ruling in 1946 ("Atlanta Negro Voters League: Strength in Unity"; "Black Suffrage"). Black Atlanta organized at once. A coalition led by the Atlanta Urban League, whose executive director was Grace Towns Hamilton, registered 14,368 new Black voters in about two months, and the All-Citizens Registration Committee, chaired by historian Clarence A. Bacote, raised the number of Black voters in the city from 6,976 to 21,244 in less than five months ("Black Suffrage"; "Clarence A. Bacote"; pg. 204). By June 1946, Black voters were more than a quarter of Atlanta's electorate ("Strength in Unity"). In 1949, John Wesley Dobbs and attorney A.T. Walden founded the Atlanta Negro Voters League to turn those numbers into political power, and John H. Calhoun Jr. and Q.V. Williamson Sr. were among its leaders ("Atlanta Negro Voters League"; pg. 185, 255).

## Discrepancies (not for the cards)

1. **Turner's birth year.** Henderson says 1831 (pg. 135); the Sheet's card says 1834–1915, the year most other sources give. The new card gives neither.
2. **Turner's seat.** Turner's ledger stone at South-View calls him "State Senator (Georgia)" (pg. 134), but lists of the Original 33 place him in the House, from Bibb County. The card says "legislature."
3. **Election date.** Henderson says Turner was elected in July 1868 (pg. 135). The *New Georgia Encyclopedia* says the members began their terms on July 4, 1868, after the spring election. The card says they took their seats that July.
4. **Slave Square count.** Henderson says about 860 people by the end of the Civil War (pg. 8); the Historic Oakland Foundation says nearly 900 by its start. The card gives no number.
5. **Ludie Andrews's dates.** Born 1872 (Georgia Women of Achievement), 1874 (other profiles) or 1875 (the AARL finding aid). She led the school until 1922 (Henderson, GSU), 1921 (Georgia Women of Achievement) or 1920 (a 1943 newspaper). The school's first class graduated in 1917 (Georgia Women of Achievement) or 1920 (AARL, GSU). The cards use "d. 1969," "until 1922" and 1920.
6. **Who ran the school after Andrews.** Henderson says Grady's white nursing officials took over in 1922; GSU and AARL say 1923. The card avoids the year.
7. **Walter White's NAACP years.** NGE says he led the national NAACP from 1929; BlackPast and others say 1931. The card gives no years.
8. **Milton's presidency.** Encyclopedia.com says Milton was president of Citizens Trust from 1926; NGE says 1938. The card says "later became its president."
9. **Federal Reserve year (Henderson error).** Henderson says Citizens Trust joined the Federal Reserve in 1936 (pg. 32). The Georgia Historical Society marker and the bank's own history say 1947, and NGE and a 2002 bank press release say 1948. The card says "the late 1940s." The FDIC first (1934) is from the marker and the bank.
10. **Hubert's sentence.** Henderson says the shooter was sentenced to twelve to fifteen years (pg. 214); the EJI marker quotes a newspaper complaining of "only two years." The card gives no sentence length.
11. **Hubert's father's church (Henderson error).** Henderson says Rev. Gaddus J. Hubert was pastor of Wheat Street Baptist (pg. 214). BlackPast's full list of Wheat Street pastors (Jackson, Tillman, Bryant 1898–1929, Henderson 1930–1937, Borders 1937–1988) has no Hubert. CNN calls him "a prominent preacher." The cards say "a prominent Baptist minister."
11a. **The tear gas attack.** Henderson says his father's church was tear-gassed during a prayer meeting during the trial; CNN, citing the Fulton County Remembrance Coalition, says a white mob tear-gassed a Black Baptist church holding a fundraiser for the family. The card follows CNN and does not name the church.
11b. **Hubert's birth year.** The CRRJ archive gives 1911; he was eighteen in June 1930, so 1911 or 1912. The person card uses 1911.
12. **The white primary ruling.** NGE's Bacote article says the U.S. Supreme Court struck down Georgia's white primary in 1946; the Black Suffrage article says the Fifth Circuit upheld a 1945 ruling (the Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal). The card says "an appeals court upheld the ruling."
13. **Who founded the Voters League.** NGE and the Atlanta History Center name John Wesley Dobbs and A.T. Walden as founders; Henderson says Calhoun co-founded it (pg. 185), and Wikipedia lists him as a later co-chair. The card names Dobbs and Walden as founders and Calhoun as one of its leaders.
14. **Starting voter count.** NGE's Bacote article starts from 6,976 Black registered voters; the Atlanta History Center says about 3,000 in 1945. The card uses NGE's figures.
