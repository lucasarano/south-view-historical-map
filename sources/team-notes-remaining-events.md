## The Odd Fellows Building Opens on Auburn Avenue (1913)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 103–4, 119, 148, 171, 234–35.
2. "Testament to Black Businesses, Odd Fellows Building Observes Centennial." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://www.ajc.com/news/local/testament-black-businesses-odd-fellows-building-observes-centennial/KZOTWB0gR8q2JV2szDe7mJ.
3. "Odd Fellows Building and Auditorium." National Register of Historic Places Nomination Form, National Park Service, 1975, https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/cccd3162-d0fa-46e3-9694-7af5919e3343.
4. "The Odd Fellow City: The Promise of a Leading Black Town." Grand United Order of Odd Fellows in America, https://guoof.org/?p=1157.
5. "Odd Fellows Building and Auditorium." HistoryPointer, NRIS 75000594, https://historypointer.com/nris/resource/75000594.

South-View Residents Involved
* Benjamin J. Davis Sr. – Led the order's Georgia lodge and its building campaign; editor of the Atlanta Independent (pg. 104).
* Robert E. Pharrow – Contractor who built the building (pg. 104, 235).
* Moses Amos – Opened the Gate City Drugstore in the building in 1914 (pg. 148).
* George Union Wilder – Odd Fellow (Past Noble Founder) killed in the 1906 massacre; the order's rings are on his gravestone (pg. 119).

Narrative for website

In the early 1900s the Grand United Order of Odd Fellows was perhaps the wealthiest Black fraternal order in Atlanta, paying sick and burial benefits to its members (pg. 103–4). Led by Benjamin J. Davis Sr., the Georgia lodge's district grand secretary and editor of the Atlanta Independent, the order built a six-story headquarters on Auburn Avenue in 1912, followed by an annex with an auditorium ("Testament to Black Businesses"; NRHP Nomination; "The Odd Fellow City"; pg. 104). Robert E. Pharrow, a Black contractor and Odd Fellow, built it, and Booker T. Washington spoke at its dedication (pg. 104; "The Odd Fellow City"). The building held offices, stores, and a roof garden, and its rents became a major source of the order's income (pg. 104). In 1914 pharmacist Moses Amos opened his Gate City Drugstore there, said to be the finest Black-owned drugstore in the South (pg. 148). Davis, Pharrow, and Amos all rest at South-View, as does George Wilder, a Civil War veteran whose gravestone carries the Odd Fellows' three linked rings (pg. 104, 119, 171, 235). The building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1975 ("Odd Fellows Building and Auditorium," HistoryPointer).

## The Great Atlanta Fire of 1917 (1917)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 182.
2. "The Great Fire of 1917." *Atlanta Magazine*, https://www.atlantamagazine.com/thisweek/this-week-in-atlanta-history-the-great-fire-o/.
3. "100 Years After the Fire." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/new-entry.
4. "Wheat Street Baptist Church." Stephen Goldfarb Photographs, Atlanta History Center, Digital Library of Georgia, https://dlg.usg.edu/record/geh_goldfarb_13.
5. "The History That Shaped the Atlanta Neighborhood Where MLK Was Raised." WABE, https://www.wabe.org/the-history-that-shaped-the-atlanta-neighborhood-where-mlk-was-raised/.

South-View Residents Involved
* Rev. Peter James Bryant – Pastor of Wheat Street Baptist Church, which burned and was rebuilt under him (pg. 182).

Narrative for website

On May 21, 1917, several fires broke out across Atlanta, and one that began in a downtown warehouse spread through the Old Fourth Ward ("The Great Fire of 1917"; "The History That Shaped the Atlanta Neighborhood"). The fire burned about 300 acres and close to 1,900 buildings, most of them homes, and left more than 10,000 people homeless ("100 Years After the Fire"; "The Great Fire of 1917"). First aid stations opened in the Odd Fellows Hall on Auburn Avenue ("The Great Fire of 1917"). The fire destroyed Wheat Street Baptist Church (pg. 182; "Wheat Street Baptist Church"). Its pastor, Rev. Peter James Bryant, led the congregation in building a new Gothic Revival church with seats for 2,500, which opened on Auburn Avenue in 1921 (pg. 182; "Wheat Street Baptist Church"). Across the neighborhood, many homeowners never rebuilt, and renters and apartment buildings took their place ("The History That Shaped the Atlanta Neighborhood").

## Mutual Federal Savings and Loan Founded (1925)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 175, 208.
2. "Our History." Sweet Auburn Works, https://www.sweetauburnworks.com/about-us/our-history/.
3. "Atlanta's Historic Auburn Ave. Again at Crossroads." Associated Press, via BET, https://www.bet.com/article/8jmtl2/atlanta-s-historic-auburn-ave-again-at-crossroads.
4. "1996 Annual Meeting: Tour Atlanta's Historic Auburn Avenue." *Perspectives on History*, American Historical Association, https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/1996-annual-meeting-tour-atlantas-historic-auburn-avenue/.
5. "Citizens Advisory Committee for Urban Renewal, Executive Committee." Ivan Allen Jr. Mayoral Records, Georgia Institute of Technology, Box 15, Folder 1, Document 87, https://ivanallen.iac.gatech.edu/mayoral-records/traditional/items/show/5513.
6. "FDIC Approves Assumption of Deposits of Mutual Federal Savings Bank of Atlanta, Atlanta, Georgia." Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, press release PR-17-2000, 10 Mar. 2000, https://archive.fdic.gov/view/fdic/1743.

South-View Residents Involved
* Jesse B. Blayton Sr. – Co-founder and later president (pg. 175).
* Clarence C. Hart – Founding investor (pg. 208). Burial at South-View not confirmed from the book text, so the card does not claim it.

Narrative for website

White-owned banks rarely made home loans to Black Atlantans, so in 1925 a group of Black businessmen who met at the Butler Street YMCA set out to lend to their own community (pg. 208; "Atlanta's Historic Auburn Ave. Again at Crossroads"). Clarence C. Hart, Atlanta's first licensed Black plumber, invested $100 to help start the Mutual Federal Building and Loan Association, and accountant Jesse B. Blayton Sr. was a co-founder (pg. 175, 208; "Our History"). Blayton later served as its president (pg. 175; "Citizens Advisory Committee for Urban Renewal"). In the early 1960s the Mutual Federal office on Auburn Avenue was the headquarters of the Citizens Student Defense Fund, which raised money for Atlanta University students who protested segregation ("Tour Atlanta's Historic Auburn Avenue"). By the 1990s Mutual Federal was one of the leading Black-owned companies in the country (pg. 208). In 2000 Citizens Trust Bank took over its deposits ("FDIC Approves Assumption"). Blayton rests at South-View (pg. 175). Note: the 1925 founding year comes from Henderson (pg. 208); outside records consulted so far confirm the association and its founders but not the year.

## King Sr.'s March on City Hall (1936)

Sources:

3. "Atlanta NAACP Leads Voter Registration Drive." Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/atlanta-naacp-leads-voter-registration-drive.
4. "King, Sr., Speaks at NAACP Mass Meeting in Atlanta." Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-sr-speaks-naacp-mass-meeting-atlanta.
5. "King, Sr., Elected to Atlanta Civic and Political League." Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-sr-elected-atlanta-civic-and-political-league.
6. "1996 Annual Meeting: Tour Atlanta's Historic Auburn Avenue." *Perspectives on History*, American Historical Association, https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/1996-annual-meeting-tour-atlantas-historic-auburn-avenue/.

South-View Residents Involved
* Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. – Led the march (pg. 220).
* John Wesley Dobbs – Founded the Atlanta Civic and Political League in 1936 (AHA tour).

Narrative for website

In 1935, Rev. Martin Luther King Sr., pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church, worked with Ruby Blackburn, Selena Sloan Butler, and the Atlanta branch of the NAACP on a voter registration drive that started on Auburn Avenue ("Atlanta NAACP Leads Voter Registration Drive"; "Tour Atlanta's Historic Auburn Avenue"). In March 1936 he spoke at an NAACP mass meeting at Wheat Street Baptist Church ("King, Sr., Speaks at NAACP Mass Meeting"). That year he led several hundred Black Atlantans on a march to City Hall to demand the right to vote (pg. 220; "Rev. Martin Luther King, Sr."). Also in 1936, John Wesley Dobbs founded the Atlanta Civic and Political League at Big Bethel AME Church, and King Sr. joined its executive committee in 1938 ("Tour Atlanta's Historic Auburn Avenue"; "King, Sr., Elected to Atlanta Civic and Political League"). King Sr. rests at South-View (pg. 115).

## The Prince Hall Masonic Temple Rises on Auburn Avenue (1937)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 100–101.
2. "Protecting the Cultural Resources." Martin Luther King, Jr. National Historical Park, National Park Service, https://www.nps.gov/malu/protecting-the-cultural-resources.htm.
3. "Prince Hall Masonic Grand Lodge." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/prince-hall-history/.
4. "Prince Hall Masonic Lodge, Once MLK's SCLC Offices, on Road to Restoration." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://www.ajc.com/uatl/prince-hall-masonic-lodge-once-mlks-sclc-offices-on-road-to-restoration/WDDHAG3EERH5NHFAUP3RNZLHZA.
5. Saporta, Maria. "Historic Preservation on Auburn Avenue." *SaportaReport*, https://saportareport.com/?p=1056114.

South-View Residents Involved
* John Wesley Dobbs – Grand master who led the building (pg. 100).
* Jesse B. Blayton Sr. – His radio station WERD broadcast from the building (pg. 175; NGE).

Narrative for website

For years Atlanta's Prince Hall Masons, the Black Masonic order, met in a temple on Ivy Street (pg. 100). In 1937, under Grand Master John Wesley Dobbs, they began building a new grand lodge at 330 Auburn Avenue, paid for with money Dobbs raised (pg. 100; "Protecting the Cultural Resources"). The building was finished in 1940, and an addition followed in 1941 ("Protecting the Cultural Resources"; "Prince Hall Masonic Grand Lodge"). Its tenants included a Madam C.J. Walker beauty school, WERD, the first radio station owned by African Americans, and the headquarters of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. ("Protecting the Cultural Resources"; "Prince Hall Masonic Lodge, Once MLK's SCLC Offices"). In 2018 the building became part of the Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park ("Historic Preservation on Auburn Avenue"). Dobbs led the Prince Hall Masons of Georgia for thirty years, until his death in 1961, and in 1963 the order marked his grave at South-View with a broken column, a monument reserved for grand masters (pg. 100–101).

## Atlanta's Tuskegee Airmen (1944)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 176, 250.
2. "1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland." CAF Rise Above, Commemorative Air Force, https://cafriseabove.org/walter-drake-westmoreland/.
3. "Horace A. Bohannon." CAF Rise Above, Commemorative Air Force, https://cafriseabove.org/?p=45307.
4. "Tuskegee Airmen." Photo caption, National Museum of the United States Air Force, https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Upcoming/Photos/igphoto/2000981892/.
5. "Wreaths Across America." Photo caption, Dobbins Air Reserve Base, U.S. Air Force, https://www.dobbins.afrc.af.mil/News/Photos/igphoto/2000298187.

South-View Residents Involved
* 1st Lt. Walter D. Westmoreland – Killed in combat, 1944 (pg. 250).
* Horace A. Bohannon – Tuskegee-trained officer-pilot (pg. 176).

Narrative for website

During World War II, the Army Air Forces trained Black pilots at Tuskegee, Alabama, and many of them flew in combat with the 332nd Fighter Group ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"; pg. 250). Two Atlanta men among them rest at South-View. Walter D. Westmoreland, a Morehouse graduate, earned his wings in July 1943 and flew a P-51C nicknamed "Dopey" with the 302nd Fighter Squadron from Italy ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"; "Tuskegee Airmen"). On October 13, 1944, enemy ground fire shot down his plane near Lake Balaton, Hungary, as he returned from an escort mission to Blechhammer, Germany, and he was killed ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"; "Tuskegee Airmen"; pg. 250). His body came home to Atlanta in 1949 (pg. 250). Horace A. Bohannon, who as a boy watched pilots at Candler Field, earned his wings in December 1944 and flew with the 332nd at Godman Field, Kentucky; the war ended before he went overseas (pg. 176; "Horace A. Bohannon"). He later helped found the Atlanta chapter of Tuskegee Airmen, Inc. ("Wreaths Across America").

## WERD, the First Black-Owned Radio Station (1949)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 175–76.
2. "WERD." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/werd.
3. "Jesse B. Blayton, Sr. (1897–1977)." BlackPast.org, https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/blayton-sr-jesse-b-1897-1977/.
4. *Atlanta Magazine*, Nov. 2023, p. 24, https://atlantamagazine.mydigitalpublication.com/atlanta-magazine-november-2023/page-24.

South-View Residents Involved
* Jesse B. Blayton Sr. – Owner (pg. 175).
* Jesse B. Blayton Jr. – Station manager for twenty years (pg. 176).

Narrative for website

In 1949 Jesse B. Blayton Sr., Georgia's first Black certified public accountant, bought Atlanta radio station WERD for $50,000, making it the first radio station in the United States owned by African Americans (pg. 175; "WERD"; "Jesse B. Blayton, Sr."). He took over the station on October 3, 1949 ("Jesse B. Blayton, Sr."). The station's first building would not let Black staff use its restrooms, so the Blaytons moved WERD to the second floor of the Prince Hall Masonic Temple on Auburn Avenue (Atlanta Magazine; "WERD"). His son, Jesse B. Blayton Jr., managed the station for twenty years (pg. 175–76; "WERD"). In the 1960s WERD shared the building with the Southern Christian Leadership Conference ("WERD"). Blayton sold the station in 1968 and was inducted into the Radio Hall of Fame in 1995 ("Jesse B. Blayton, Sr."; pg. 175). Blayton and his son rest at South-View (pg. 175–76).

## Atlanta's 1973 City Charter (1973)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 185, 205, 216.

4. "Grace Towns Hamilton Papers, 1900–1992." Robert W. Woodruff Library, Atlanta University Center, finding aid, https://findingaids.auctr.edu/repositories/2/resources/144.
5. "Hamilton, Grace Towns." Atlanta History Center, via UMBRA Search, https://www.umbrasearch.org/catalog/edef50a30e6b4b852720db33ce9cafed0ececcd0.
6. "ATLVault: Maynard Jackson Made American History, 50 Years Ago." Atlanta News First, 16 Oct. 2023, https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2023/10/16/hfr-atlvault-maynard-jackson-made-american-history-50-years-ago.

South-View Residents Involved
* Grace Towns Hamilton – Principal architect (pg. 205).
* John H. Calhoun Jr. – Elected to the City Council in 1973 (pg. 185).

Narrative for website

In 1973 Atlanta replaced its century-old city charter, and State Representative Grace Towns Hamilton was a principal architect of the new one (pg. 205; "Grace Towns Hamilton," NGE). The new charter made the mayor the head of a stronger executive branch, cut back the number of council seats elected citywide, and added seats elected by district ("Grace Towns Hamilton Papers," AHC; "Hamilton, Grace Towns," AHC). For the first time, Black Atlantans were elected to the City Council in numbers that matched their share of the population (pg. 205; "Grace Towns Hamilton Papers," AUC; "Grace Towns Hamilton," NGE). In 1973 John H. Calhoun Jr., at seventy-four, won a seat on the council (pg. 185). That same year Atlanta elected Maynard Jackson its first Black mayor (pg. 216; "ATLVault: Maynard Jackson"). Hamilton and Calhoun rest at South-View (pg. 185, 205).

## Maynard Jackson Elected Mayor (1973)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 100–101, 129, 216.
2. "ATLVault: Maynard Jackson Made American History, 50 Years Ago." Atlanta News First, 16 Oct. 2023, https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2023/10/16/hfr-atlvault-maynard-jackson-made-american-history-50-years-ago.
3. "Jackson, Maynard, 1938–2003." Social Networks and Archival Context (SNAC), https://snaccooperative.org/view/5420347.
4. "Ex-Atlanta Mayor Jackson Dies." CBS News, June 2003, https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ex-atlanta-mayor-jackson-dies/.
5. "Monument for Mayor Jackson Oversized and Grand, but Was Maynard." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://ajc.com/news/local/monument-for-mayor-jackson-oversized-and-grand-but-was-maynard/m3eZzq1KcLXb1r8xtHBk5K.

South-View Residents Involved
* John Wesley Dobbs – His grandfather (pg. 100).
* Irene Dobbs Jackson – His mother (pg. 216). Maynard Jackson himself rests at Oakland Cemetery.

Narrative for website

On October 16, 1973, Atlanta voters elected Maynard Jackson Jr., thirty-five years old, as the city's first Black mayor and the first Black mayor of a major Southern city, with about 60 percent of the vote ("ATLVault: Maynard Jackson"; "Jackson, Maynard, 1938–2003"). Four years earlier he had become Atlanta's first Black vice mayor ("Jackson, Maynard, 1938–2003"; "ATLVault: Maynard Jackson"). He was the grandson of John Wesley Dobbs, the "Mayor of Auburn Avenue," and the son of Irene Dobbs Jackson, who in 1959 became the first Black Atlantan to receive a card from the main public library (pg. 100, 216). Jackson won a second term in 1977 and a third in 1989 ("ATLVault: Maynard Jackson"; "Ex-Atlanta Mayor Jackson Dies"). He died in 2003 and rests at Oakland Cemetery, not South-View; his grandfather and mother rest at South-View ("Jackson, Maynard, 1938–2003"; "Monument for Mayor Jackson"; pg. 101, 129).

## People

### Benjamin J. Davis Sr. (1870–1945; year on map 1913)

Benjamin Jefferson Davis Sr. (1870–1945) joined the Odd Fellows at seventeen, while a student at Atlanta University, and became district grand secretary of the order's Georgia lodge, one of its leaders (pg. 104; "The Odd Fellow City"). In 1903 he founded the Atlanta Independent, a weekly that served as the order's newspaper, and used it to protest racial violence and push Black Atlantans toward self-reliance ("The Atlanta Independent: 1915"; pg. 104). He was a national committeeman of the Republican Party and an ally of Booker T. Washington (pg. 104). As the order's leader he made death benefits of $300 to $500 mandatory for every member so that widows and orphans would be cared for, and he led the building of the Odd Fellows headquarters on Auburn Avenue (pg. 104; "Testament to Black Businesses"). He rests at South-View.

### Robert E. Pharrow (Died 1944; year on map 1913)

Robert Edward Pharrow (d. 1944) was one of the most successful Black building contractors in the country and taught himself civil and mechanical engineering (pg. 234). He started his business in Birmingham, Alabama, then built some of Black Atlanta's best-known buildings, including Flipper Hall at Morris Brown College, Sale Hall at Morehouse, the First Congregational Church, and the six-story Odd Fellows Building on Auburn Avenue (pg. 104, 235; "The Odd Fellow City"). He was an Odd Fellow, a Mason, an Elk, and a Knight of Pythias, a director of the Atlanta State Savings Bank, and a trustee of Morris Brown College (pg. 235). He rests at South-View.

### Lucius Henry Holsey (1842–1920; year on map 1927)

Lucius Henry Holsey (1842–1920) was born enslaved near Columbus, Georgia, and was largely self-educated ("Bishop Lucius Henry Holsey, 1882"; "Bishop Lucius Henry Holsey"). He helped organize the Colored Methodist Episcopal Church (now Christian Methodist Episcopal) after the Civil War, became one of its bishops in 1873, and led the founding of Paine College in Augusta (pg. 132; "Lucius Holsey"; "Holsey, Lucius Henry"). He lived on Auburn Avenue and died in Atlanta in 1920 ("Lucius Holsey"). On November 17, 1927, about five hundred people from across the South came to South-View to unveil a marble monument to him, paid for by the preachers and members of the church in Georgia (pg. 132–33). Its inscription calls him a "bishop, orator, lecturer and eloquent preacher of the gospel" (pg. 133). He rests at South-View.

### Walter D. Westmoreland (1920–1944; year on map 1944)

First Lieutenant Walter Drake Westmoreland (1920–1944) was born in Atlanta, a nephew of NAACP leader Walter White ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"). He graduated from Morehouse College, earned a master's degree at Atlanta University, and won his pilot's wings at Tuskegee in July 1943 (pg. 250; "1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"). Flying a P-51C fighter nicknamed "Dopey" with the 302nd Fighter Squadron of the 332nd Fighter Group, he flew bomber escort missions ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"). On October 13, 1944, enemy ground fire shot down his plane near Lake Balaton, Hungary, and he was killed ("1st Lt Walter Drake Westmoreland"). When his body came home to Atlanta in 1949, his father, William G. Westmoreland, had died days before, and the family held a double funeral at First Congregational Church (pg. 250). He rests at South-View.

### Horace A. Bohannon (1922–2003; year on map 1944)

Horace Augustus Bohannon (1922–2003) grew up poor in Atlanta, took his first job at eight to help his family, and fell in love with flying watching pilots at Candler Field ("Horace A. Bohannon"; pg. 176). After civilian pilot training at Lincoln University, he worked at the new Tuskegee Army Air Field, then earned his own wings in December 1944 and served as an officer-pilot with the 332nd Fighter Group at Godman Field, Kentucky (pg. 176; "Horace A. Bohannon"). After the war he ran the Southern Regional Council's Georgia Veterans Service Project for Black veterans, finished his degree at Lincoln in 1948, taught for fifteen years in the Fulton County schools, and became the first Black regional Management Training Officer for the U.S. Post Office (pg. 176; "Horace A. Bohannon Oral History Interview"). He led drives to raise statues of educator Charles L. Harper and YMCA leader Joseph D. Winston (pg. 176). He rests at South-View.

## Discrepancies and notes for the team

1. **Odd Fellows Building dates.** Henderson (pg. 104) says the building was dedicated in 1913 at Big Bethel with Booker T. Washington speaking, and the annex came the next year. The National Register nomination and the AJC give 1912 for the office building and 1913 for the auditorium; the GUOOF history and an Atlanta Daily World centennial piece put the grand opening in 1913 and the auditorium opening in 1914. The card gives 1912 for construction and avoids a dedication year. Dates field: 1912–1914.
2. **Benjamin J. Davis Sr.'s title.** Henderson (pg. 104) calls him the order's "grand director"; the GUOOF history calls him district grand secretary. The cards use district grand secretary.
3. **Mutual Federal founding year (needs one more source).** The 1925 date comes only from Henderson (pg. 208). Outside sources confirm the founders, purpose, Blayton's presidency and the 2000 takeover, but none gave a founding year. Henderson also calls it the Mutual Federal *Building and Loan* Association; later records say *Savings and Loan*. Grant chose to include it with a note on the card. Still worth having someone check Calloway's *Sweet Auburn Avenue Business History* (1988) at the Auburn Avenue Research Library.
4. **Year of King Sr.'s march.** Henderson (pg. 220) and 11Alive say 1936. The King Institute chronology places King Sr.'s NAACP registration drive in August–September 1935, and the AHA tour credits a 1935 campaign. Some accounts may put the march in 1935. Dates field: 1935–1936. Also: one source (KUT, on *Daddy King*) describes a City Hall march over separate water fountains; it is unclear whether that is the same march, so the card does not mention it.
5. **Prince Hall Temple dates and address.** Henderson (pg. 100) says the temple was erected in 1937. NPS says it was designed in 1937 and finished in 1940; the Atlanta History Center says built 1937 with an addition in 1941; the AJC says built 1940 in one place and 1937 in another. Address is given as 330 (NPS, AHC) or 334 (NGE) Auburn Avenue. The card uses 330 and "began building in 1937, finished 1940, addition 1941".
6. **Westmoreland's unit and Anzio.** Henderson (pg. 250) writes "the 332nd fighter squadron of the 302nd Group"; it was the reverse: 302nd Fighter Squadron, 332nd Fighter Group (CAF, NMUSAF). Henderson also says he was shot down once before while supporting the Anzio beach landing; CAF records only a July 1, 1944 belly landing at Ramitelli, Italy. The Anzio story is left off the cards.
7. **Bohannon's statue.** Henderson (pg. 176) names the Booker T. Washington principal "Charles S. Harper"; Henderson's own entry (pg. 206) and other sources give Charles Lincoln Harper. The card says Charles L. Harper. Henderson says Bohannon helped *build* Tuskegee Army Air Field; CAF says he *worked* there (in the control tower office). The card says "worked at".
8. **Holsey monument.** The 1927 unveiling details come only from Henderson (pg. 132–33, citing the *Atlanta Constitution*, 28 July 1927). That is why Holsey is a person card (one source is enough) and not a separate event. Henderson calls it the first monument of its kind erected by Black Americans to a church leader; the card leaves that claim out. Paine College's founding is dated 1882, 1883 or 1884 depending on the source; the card gives no year.
9. **Dobbs Avenue renaming.** Henderson (pg. 100) says Houston Street was renamed for Dobbs in 1994 "during the administration of his grandson." Jackson's last term ended in early January 1994, so the renaming may have been approved in late 1993. Left off the Jackson card.
10. **Sheet housekeeping.** Entries row 124 currently holds a placeholder named "Person 1 " (with a trailing space). This batch starts at row 125 so it doesn't overwrite it. The Entries tab also has duplicate rows for "David T. Howard", "Frank Smith" and "Bishop Henry McNeal Turner" with blank years. Worth a team check.
11. **Not included.** No living people are named. Clarence C. Hart (d. 1995) is named on the Mutual Federal card only. Maynard Jackson has no person card because he rests at Oakland; his card says so.
