## Civil Rights Movement (1954–1968, the struggle continues today) · updates the existing entry

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018.
2. Tuck, Stephen. "Civil Rights Movement." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/civil-rights-movement/.
3. "Major King Events Chronology: 1929–1968." The Martin Luther King, Jr., Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-resources/major-king-events-chronology-1929-1968.

South-View Residents Involved
* Every person in these notes, plus Irene Dobbs Jackson and John Wesley Dobbs, who are already on the map.

Narrative for website

The struggle for civil rights is not a self-contained chapter in history, but a continuous story that connects the era of Reconstruction through the 1960s to the present day. To understand this movement fully, we must look beyond the most celebrated figures and examine both nationwide efforts and the deeply rooted local activism in cities like Atlanta.

While leaders such as Martin Luther King Jr., Malcolm X, Rosa Parks, Thurgood Marshall, and John Lewis helped shape the public consciousness, countless less heralded figures—including Ralph David Abernathy Sr., Joseph Lowery, Herman Russell, C.T. Vivian, Hosea Williams, Andrew Young Jr., Julian Bond, Joseph E. Boone, Lonnie C. King Jr., and Dr. Roslyn Pope—fought tirelessly against everyday indignities. Organizations as different as the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, and the Black Panther Party carried the struggle forward in their own ways.

Many of the people who carried this struggle in Atlanta now rest at South-View, the cemetery that formerly enslaved Black Atlantans founded in 1886 so their families could bury their dead with dignity. Among them are ministers William Holmes Borders and Samuel W. Williams, student organizers Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson and Fred C. Bennette Jr., businessmen Jesse Hill Jr. and Herman J. Russell, Martin Luther King Sr. and Alberta Williams King, and Congressman John Lewis. Dr. King himself was first laid to rest here in 1968 (pg. 6, 114–15).

From the Montgomery Bus Boycotts and sit-ins to pivotal marches across historic locations like the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma and the March on Washington, these events transformed the nation. By honoring both the major milestones and the smaller, overlooked personal stories, we preserve a richer, more accurate history of the ongoing fight for equality.

## Triple L Movement (1957 – 1959)

Sources:
1. Hatfield, Edward A. "Bus Desegregation in Atlanta." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/bus-desegregation-in-atlanta/.
2. "From William Holmes Borders." The Martin Luther King, Jr., Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, 6 Apr. 1957, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/william-holmes-borders.
3. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 177, 254.

South-View Residents Involved
* Rev. William Holmes Borders – Led the Triple L Movement from Wheat Street Baptist Church (pg. 177).
* Rev. Samuel W. Williams – One of the plaintiffs in the successful 1959 lawsuit that desegregated Atlanta's city buses (pg. 254).

Historical Notes
* In January 1957, after the Montgomery Bus Boycott, a group of Black ministers launched the Love, Law, and Liberation (Triple L) Movement to desegregate Atlanta's city buses. (Source 1)
* On January 8, 1957, Borders announced the plan to more than 1,200 people at Wheat Street Baptist Church on Auburn Avenue. (Source 1)
* On January 9, six ministers boarded a downtown bus at Mitchell and Whitehall streets and took the front seats. Police arrested them the following afternoon, giving the ministers the court test case they wanted, and the ministers then asked other Black riders not to repeat the protest while the case went forward. (Sources 1, 2)
* On January 9, 1959, the federal district court in Atlanta ruled for the ministers. On January 20, Borders told an audience of 2,500 that riders could now sit where they wanted. (Source 1)

Narrative for website

In Atlanta, Black riders were made to sit at the back of city buses. In January 1957, soon after the Montgomery Bus Boycott, a group of Black ministers led by Rev. William Holmes Borders of Wheat Street Baptist Church launched the Love, Law, and Liberation (Triple L) Movement to change that. On January 8, Borders told a crowd of more than 1,200 at Wheat Street, "We are going to ride until these buses are desegregated" ("Bus Desegregation in Atlanta"). The next day, six ministers boarded a downtown bus and took the front seats, and police arrested them the following afternoon, creating the test case the ministers had planned for. Two years later, on January 9, 1959, a federal court ruled in their favor, and Atlanta's buses opened to every rider ("Bus Desegregation in Atlanta"). Borders and Rev. Samuel W. Williams, one of the plaintiffs in the bus case, both rest at South-View (pg. 177, 254).

## Atlanta Student Movement (1960 – 1961)

Sources:
1. Hatfield, Edward A. "Atlanta Sit-ins." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/atlanta-sit-ins/.
2. King, Lonnie C., Jr. "Atlanta Student Movement Timeline." Civil Rights Movement Archive, 2013, https://www.crmvet.org/info/60asmtim.htm.
3. "The Atlanta Student Movement." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/exhibitions/the-color-line-the-problem-of-the-centuries/students-and-strategy/.
4. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 174, 177, 212, 219, 238.

South-View Residents Involved
* Fred C. Bennette Jr. – Led the sit-in campaign to desegregate Rich's department store while still a student at Morris Brown College (pg. 174).
* Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson – Joined the sit-ins as a Spelman student (pg. 238).
* Rev. William Holmes Borders – Chaired the committee of adults who advised the student protesters (pg. 177).
* Jesse Hill Jr. – Helped launch the *Atlanta Inquirer* in the summer of 1960 as a voice of protest (pg. 212).
* Rev. A.D. Williams King – Arrested with his brother at the October 19, 1960 sit-ins (Source 1).
* Julian Bond – A Morehouse student protest leader who helped organize SNCC in 1960 (pg. 177). Remembered at South-View.

Historical Notes
* In February 1960, inspired by the Greensboro, North Carolina, sit-ins, students at Atlanta's six historically Black colleges began organizing. In March they formed the Committee on Appeal for Human Rights (COAHR). (Sources 1, 2)
* On March 9, 1960, the students published "An Appeal for Human Rights" as a full-page ad in the *Atlanta Constitution*, *Atlanta Journal*, and *Atlanta Daily World*. (Source 1)
* On March 15, 1960, nearly 200 students sat in at ten downtown lunch counters and cafeterias. Police arrested 77 of them. (Source 1)
* On October 19, 1960, students began a new round of sit-ins that included the whites-only Magnolia Room restaurant at Rich's, Atlanta's largest department store. Police arrested more than fifty people that day, including Martin Luther King Jr. and his brother A.D. King. The next day more than 2,000 students closed sixteen more lunch counters. (Source 1)
* On March 7, 1961, student leaders Lonnie King and Herschelle Sullivan learned that the city's white and Black leaders had agreed to desegregate the lunch counters the following fall, after the schools desegregated. The students accepted reluctantly. The lunch counters opened to Black customers in October 1961. (Source 1)
* Full desegregation of Atlanta's public places did not come until the Civil Rights Act of 1964. (Source 1)

Narrative for website

In 1960, Black Atlantans could shop at downtown stores like Rich's but were refused a seat at their lunch counters and restaurants. That February, students at Atlanta's six historically Black colleges began to organize, and on March 9 they published "An Appeal for Human Rights" in all three of the city's daily newspapers ("Atlanta Sit-ins"). Six days later, nearly 200 students sat in at ten downtown lunch counters, and police arrested 77 of them. On October 19, students returned, this time to Rich's whites-only Magnolia Room, and police arrested more than fifty people, including Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and his brother A.D. King ("Atlanta Sit-ins"). Fred C. Bennette Jr., a Morris Brown student, led the Rich's campaign (pg. 174); Rev. William Holmes Borders chaired the adults who advised the students (pg. 177); and Jesse Hill Jr. helped launch the *Atlanta Inquirer* as a newspaper of protest (pg. 212). In March 1961 the city's leaders agreed to open the lunch counters, and they did so that October ("Atlanta Sit-ins").

## Dr. King Laid to Rest at South-View (1968 – 1970)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 6, 69, 113–15.
2. "Plan Your Visit." The King Center, https://thekingcenter.org/visit/.
3. "What is South-View Cemetery? Here's the history where John Lewis is buried." WSB-TV, 2020, https://www.wsbtv.com/news/local/atlanta/what-is-south-view-cemetery-heres-history-where-john-lewis-is-buried/CSKPZVG6PFFJXKD3XKCOPKRP2I/.

South-View Residents Involved
* Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. and Alberta Williams King – Dr. King's parents, later buried in the same crypt where their son first rested (pg. 115).
* Albert H. Watts – South-View's president, grandson of founder Albert Watts, shown standing at Dr. King's gravesite (pg. 114, photo caption).

Historical Notes
* After Dr. King was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968, a farm wagon drawn by mules carried his casket to South-View. (Source 2)
* His burial drew the largest gathering in South-View's history; photographers stood on top of nearby mausoleums to take pictures. (pg. 114)
* The Robert Shields Memorial Company designed his marble crypt. Because it was needed so quickly, the inscription was stenciled at first rather than carved. An eternal gas flame was later added, and a guard kept watch. (pg. 115)
* His crypt stood on "mausoleum row," the line of above-ground tombs along Jonesboro Road to the right of the entrance gate. (pg. 69)
* On January 15, 1969, what would have been his fortieth birthday, about three hundred people gathered at South-View for a memorial service, and thousands more visited his grave that year. (pg. 115)
* In January 1970 his body was moved to a site near Ebenezer Baptist Church, now the King Center. (pg. 115; Sources 2, 3)
* His parents were later buried in the same crypt at South-View. (pg. 115)

Narrative for website

On April 9, 1968, five days after he was assassinated in Memphis, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. was carried on a mule-drawn farm wagon to South-View Cemetery ("Plan Your Visit"). His burial drew the largest gathering in South-View's history; photographers climbed onto nearby mausoleums to see over the crowd (pg. 114). He was laid to rest in a marble crypt on mausoleum row, its inscription stenciled at first because it was needed so quickly, and an eternal flame and a guard were later added (pg. 69, 115). On what would have been his fortieth birthday, January 15, 1969, about three hundred people gathered at his grave, and thousands more came that year. In January 1970 his body was moved to a tomb near Ebenezer Baptist Church, now part of the King Center (pg. 115). His parents, Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. and Alberta Williams King, were later laid to rest at South-View in the same crypt where their son first rested (pg. 115).

## Hank Aaron's 715th Home Run (1974)

Sources:
1. Jaffe, Jay. "Watch: Hank Aaron hits record-breaking 715th homer 40 years ago today." *Sports Illustrated*, 8 Apr. 2014, https://www.si.com/mlb/strike-zone/2014/04/08/video-hank-aaron-715th-homer-40th-anniversary-babe-ruth.

3. "Hank Aaron's funeral a tribute to more than a baseball great." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, 28 Jan. 2021, https://ajc.com/sports/hank-aarons-funeral-a-tribute-to-more-than-a-baseball-great/NTMY77KPAFCWFHKNE4LJRBSGS4/.

South-View Residents Involved
* Henry "Hank" Aaron – Broke Babe Ruth's record in Atlanta; laid to rest at South-View in 2021 (Sources 3, 4).

Historical Notes
* Aaron ended the 1973 season with 713 home runs, one short of Babe Ruth's record. Over the winter he received racist hate mail and threats and needed bodyguards and police protection on road trips. (Source 1)
* On April 8, 1974, in Atlanta, he hit his 715th home run off the Los Angeles Dodgers' Al Downing. He finished his career with 755. (Source 1)
* Teammates recalled fearing that someone would try to shoot him at home plate; threats brought police cars to his home and his oldest daughter home from college. (Source 2)
* Aaron married Billye Williams, the widow of Rev. Samuel W. Williams. (pg. 254)
* His funeral was held at Friendship Baptist Church, where Rev. Williams had been pastor, on January 27, 2021, and he was laid to rest at South-View, first in a temporary crypt and then, in January 2022, at his permanent gravesite. (Sources 3, 4; pg. 254)

Narrative for website

Hank Aaron ended the 1973 season one home run short of Babe Ruth's record of 714. That winter he received racist hate mail and threats on his life, and he needed bodyguards and police protection to keep playing ("Watch: Hank Aaron"). His teammates feared someone would shoot him on the field (Odum). On April 8, 1974, in front of a home crowd in Atlanta, he hit his 715th home run off the Dodgers' Al Downing and took the record, and he finished his career with 755 ("Watch: Hank Aaron"). Aaron married Billye Williams, the widow of Rev. Samuel W. Williams (pg. 254). In January 2021 his funeral was held at Friendship Baptist Church, where Rev. Williams had once been pastor, and he was laid to rest at South-View ("Hank Aaron's funeral").

## The Green Book in Atlanta (1936 – 1966)

Sources:
1. "Atlanta in the Negro Motorist Green Book." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/atlanta-in-the-negro-motorist-green-book/.
2. "In Segregation-Era Atlanta, These Places Welcomed Travelers." WABE, https://wabe.org/?p=45670.
3. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 206.

South-View Residents Involved
* John Harden and Willie Lee "Billie" Harden – Owned Harden's Service Station, one of the few Atlanta stations listed in the Green Book (Source 1; pg. 206).

Historical Notes
* Victor Hugo Green, a Harlem postal worker, published the Green Book from 1936 to 1966. It listed hotels, restaurants, bars, and service stations where Black travelers would be served, at a time of segregation, sundown towns, and lynching. (Sources 1, 2)
* Harden's Service Station on Hunter Street was one of the few places in Atlanta listed in the Green Book where Black travelers could safely buy gas and get repairs. Members of the Atlanta Black Crackers baseball team, which the Hardens bought in 1937, gathered there. (Source 1)
* John Harden was the first African American to own and operate a gas station in Atlanta. He opened his Hunter Street station around 1930 and a second station on Auburn Avenue in 1935. (pg. 206; Source 3)

Narrative for website

Under segregation, a Black family driving through the South could be turned away at a gas station, a restaurant, or a hotel, and some towns were unsafe for them after dark. From 1936 to 1966, Victor Hugo Green, a Harlem postal worker, published the Green Book, a guide to the businesses where Black travelers would be served ("In Segregation-Era Atlanta"). In Atlanta, one of the few gas stations listed was Harden's Service Station on Hunter Street, owned by John and Willie Lee "Billie" Harden, where Black drivers could safely fill up and get repairs ("Atlanta in the Negro Motorist Green Book"). John Harden was the first African American to own and operate a gas station in Atlanta, and the Hardens also owned the Atlanta Black Crackers baseball team (pg. 206). Both rest at South-View.

## People

* **Rev. William Holmes Borders (1905–1993)**, a third-generation minister, served as pastor of Wheat Street Baptist Church on Auburn Avenue from 1937 until 1988, and the church grew more under his leadership than at any other time in its history (pg. 177). He helped lead early civil rights efforts in Atlanta, including the push to desegregate the Atlanta Police Department in the 1940s and the campaign to desegregate the city's buses in the 1950s (pg. 177). In January 1957 he led the Love, Law, and Liberation (Triple L) Movement, telling a crowd at Wheat Street, "We are going to ride until these buses are desegregated" ("Bus Desegregation in Atlanta"). During the student sit-ins of the 1960s, he chaired the committee of adults who advised the students (pg. 177). In the 1960s and 1970s he led Wheat Street in building low-income housing at Wheat Street Gardens and housing for older residents at Wheat Street Towers, and he used radio and television to build support for civil rights (pg. 178). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 177–78; Hatfield, "Bus Desegregation in Atlanta," *New Georgia Encyclopedia*.

* **Rev. Samuel Woodrow Williams (1912–1970)**, an Arkansas native, earned degrees at Morehouse College and Howard University and doctoral degrees at the University of Chicago and Arkansas Baptist College (pg. 254). He was pastor of Friendship Baptist Church from 1954 until his death and chaired the philosophy and religion department at Morehouse College (pg. 254). He served as president of the Atlanta branch of the NAACP, and in 1959 he was one of the plaintiffs in the successful lawsuit that desegregated Atlanta's city buses (pg. 254). He also held leadership roles in the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, the Atlanta Summit Leadership Conference, the Greater Atlanta Council on Human Relations, and the Georgia Council on Human Relations (pg. 254). His widow, Billye Williams, later married baseball player Hank Aaron (pg. 254). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 254.

* **Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. (1899–1984)** was pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church for forty-four years (pg. 220). Long before his son became a national leader, he was active in the Atlanta Civic and Political League and the NAACP, led a voting-rights march on Atlanta City Hall in 1936, and pressed for equal pay for Black teachers (pg. 220). He served on the boards of Atlanta University and Morehouse College (pg. 220). His son, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., was first laid to rest at South-View in 1968, and King Sr. and his wife, Alberta Williams King, were later buried in the same crypt where their son had first rested (pg. 115). His epitaph reads "I love Everyone" and "Still in Business, Just Moved Upstairs" (pg. 220).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 115, 220.

* **Alberta Williams King (1904–1974).** "Her life shall be filled with music," reads her epitaph at South-View (pg. 220). The daughter of Rev. Adam Daniel Williams and Jennie Celeste Parks Williams, she grew up in Ebenezer Baptist Church, where her father was pastor, and she started the church's choir and served as its organist and music director (pg. 220, 254). She attended Spelman Seminary, earned a teaching certificate at Hampton Normal Institute, and taught school before marrying Martin Luther King Sr., who followed her father as pastor of Ebenezer (pg. 220). She was the mother of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. In 1974, a gunman killed her as she played "The Lord's Prayer" during Sunday worship at Ebenezer (pg. 220). She rests at South-View beside her husband (pg. 69, 115).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 69, 115, 220, 254.

* **Rev. Alfred Daniel Williams King (1930–1969)**, the younger brother of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., worked beside his brother in the movement. On October 19, 1960, he was among the more than fifty people arrested in the Atlanta student sit-ins that included Rich's Magnolia Room ("Atlanta Sit-ins"). During the 1963 Birmingham campaign he pastored First Baptist Church of Ensley, also recorded as First Street Baptist Church, and on May 11, 1963, his family's home in Ensley was bombed; the family escaped ("Alabama: A.D. King Home"; pg. 219). In Louisville, Kentucky, where he pastored Zion Baptist Church, he led protests that eventually won an open housing law in Kentucky (pg. 219). He served on the board of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and worked in the Poor People's Campaign, and after his brother's death he became co-pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church with his father (pg. 219). He drowned in the swimming pool at his Atlanta home in 1969; the coroner found no foul play (pg. 219). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 219; Hatfield, "Atlanta Sit-ins," *New Georgia Encyclopedia*; "Alabama: A.D. King Home." National Park Service, https://www.nps.gov/places/alabama-a-d-king-home.htm; "Birmingham Home of A.D. King Added to Civil Rights Network," *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://www.ajc.com/news/birmingham-home-of-ad-king-added-to-civil-rights-network/M7PXLQOKKFC75ORBYBQZMIVMAE/ (family escaped).

* **Rev. Fred C. Bennette Jr. (1928–1994)** graduated from Morris Brown College and Turner Theological Seminary (pg. 174). While still a student at Morris Brown, he led the sit-in campaign to desegregate Rich's department store (pg. 174). He went on to direct voter registration drives for the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), serve as deputy director of the Georgia Voters League, and become the first director of SCLC's Operation Breadbasket, which organized Black churches to challenge the hiring practices of businesses that would not hire Black workers (pg. 174). He was one of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s closest and most trusted aides, later advised Georgia Governor Jimmy Carter and Atlanta Mayor Andrew Young, and served as security director of the King Center (pg. 174). His gravestone at South-View bears the motto "Commandante," and Andrew Young wrote his epitaph: "You were always there when Martin needed you" (pg. 174).
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 174; "Fred C. Bennette, Jr. Collection," Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History, finding aid, 2004, https://aspace-gpls.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/3/resources/242 (born December 2, 1928).

* **Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson (1942–1967)** grew up in Atlanta's Summerhill neighborhood and became active in the movement while still a teenager (pg. 238). As a Spelman College student she took part in the sit-ins (pg. 238). After a "jail, no bail" protest in South Carolina, she and other students served thirty days, including time on a chain gang, and during the Freedom Rides she served forty-five days in Mississippi's Parchman Penitentiary (pg. 238). She organized chapters of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and became the first and only woman to serve as its executive secretary (pg. 238). Her health failed, and she died of cancer at twenty-five (pg. 238). Her epitaph at South-View reads, "If you think free, you are free" (pg. 238).
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 238.

* **Julian Bond (1940–2015)** became a student protest leader while attending Morehouse College, and in 1960 he helped organize the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), serving as its first communications director (pg. 177). Atlanta voters elected him to the Georgia House of Representatives in 1965, but in January 1966 the House voted to bar him from his seat because he supported SNCC's statement against the Vietnam War; the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously in December 1966 that the House had violated his right to free speech, and he took his seat (*Bond v. Floyd*). He later served in the Georgia Senate, co-founded the Southern Poverty Law Center and served as its president, and led the NAACP, first as president of its Atlanta branch and later as chairman of the national organization (pg. 177). He was also a television commentator, newspaper columnist, poet, and college professor (pg. 177). He is remembered at South-View, where a marker carries his epitaph, "Easily Amused Race Man." His parents, educator Horace Mann Bond and librarian Julia Washington Bond, also rest at South-View (pg. 177).
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 177; *Bond v. Floyd*, 385 U.S. 116 (1966).
  * Sheet: Rests at South-View = "Memorial at South-View" (not "Yes") until Ms. Hemphill confirms.

* **John Lewis (1940–2020)** joined the sit-in movement as a college student in Nashville, and in 1961 he rode in the Freedom Rides, where white segregationists attacked him and other riders at the bus station in Montgomery, Alabama (Moye). He moved to Atlanta in 1963 as chair of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and made the city his home after he left SNCC in 1966 (Moye). In 1965, Alabama state troopers beat Lewis and some 600 marchers as he led them across the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, on the day remembered as "Bloody Sunday" (Moye). He won an at-large seat on the Atlanta City Council in 1981 and then represented Georgia's Fifth Congressional District in the U.S. House for more than thirty years (Moye). He died on July 17, 2020, and on July 30 he was laid to rest at South-View beside his wife, Lillian Miles Lewis, a librarian at Atlanta University who was his chief adviser in his successful campaign for Congress ("John Lewis: A Founding Father"; pg. 223).
  * Sources: Moye, J. T. "John Lewis (1940–2020)." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, 20 July 2020, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/government-politics/john-lewis-1940-2020/; "John Lewis: A Founding Father of a better America," *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, 30 July 2020, https://www.ajc.com/john-lewis/john-lewis-a-founding-father-of-a-better-america/2PYQEPHYIRH7NE4R6B5SS2JPZU/; Henderson, p. 223.

* **Jesse Hill Jr. (1926–2012)** earned an MBA in actuarial science at the University of Michigan and became the third president of Atlanta Life Insurance Company, the company Alonzo Herndon founded (pg. 210–11). In the summer of 1960 he helped launch the *Atlanta Inquirer* newspaper "as a voice of protest against racial discrimination" and as an outlet for positive coverage of the movement (pg. 212). Though he never held political office, he advised Atlanta mayors from Sam Massell to Kasim Reed, as well as Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and Governor Jimmy Carter, and he was known for building alliances between Black and white leaders (pg. 211). He became the first Black president of the Metro Atlanta Chamber of Commerce, the first Black member and later chair of the Board of Regents of Georgia's university system, and chair of the King Center's board (pg. 212). Part of Butler Street now carries his name (pg. 212). He rests at South-View (pg. 6).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 6, 210–12.

* **Herman Jerome Russell (1930–2014)** was born in Atlanta during the Great Depression and began shining shoes and selling Coca-Colas as a child; by sixteen he had saved $125 to buy his first piece of property, and the rent from it paid for his college education (pg. 239). After earning a degree in construction at Tuskegee, he built H. J. Russell and Company, which helped build much of Atlanta's skyline (pg. 239). During the height of the movement he paid bail bonds for jailed activists and was a major donor to the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (pg. 239). He was the first Black member of the Metro Atlanta Chamber of Commerce and its second Black president, after his close friend Jesse Hill Jr. (pg. 239). He rests at South-View (pg. 6).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 6, 239.

* **Jesse Bee Blayton Sr. (1897–1977)**, a World War I veteran, became the first Black certified public accountant in Georgia in 1928 (pg. 175). He taught accounting and economics at Morehouse College, Atlanta University, and Morris Brown College, co-founded the Mutual Federal Savings and Loan Association, which made home loans to Black families, and served as cashier and vice president of Citizens Trust Bank (pg. 175). In 1949 he bought WERD, the first radio station in the United States owned by African Americans; his son, J. B. Blayton Jr., managed the station for twenty years (pg. 175–76). With Clayton Yates and Lorimer Milton he also owned the Top Hat Club on Auburn Avenue (pg. 192). He was inducted into the Radio Hall of Fame in 1995 (pg. 175). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 175–76, 192.

* **John Harden (d. 1979) and Willie Lee "Billie" Harden (d. 1991).** John Harden was the first African American to own and operate a gas station in Atlanta (pg. 206). He opened the Harden Service Station on Hunter Street around 1930 and bought a second station on Auburn Avenue in 1935, and he kept it open twenty-four hours a day, closing only once in thirty years, on the day President John F. Kennedy was assassinated (pg. 206). Harden's was one of the few stations in Atlanta listed in the Green Book where Black travelers could safely buy gas and get repairs ("Atlanta in the Negro Motorist Green Book"). John and Billie Harden also co-owned and managed the Atlanta Black Crackers baseball team, which they bought in 1937 (pg. 206; "Atlanta in the Negro Motorist Green Book"). Both rest at South-View, where their mausoleum carries the family name.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 206; "Atlanta in the Negro Motorist Green Book," Atlanta History Center. Mausoleum: South-View's Georgia Tech tour handout (photo of the Harden mausoleum).

* **Henry "Hank" Aaron (1934–2021)** ended the 1973 season one home run short of Babe Ruth's record and spent that winter receiving racist hate mail and threats, needing bodyguards and police protection to keep playing ("Watch: Hank Aaron"). On April 8, 1974, in Atlanta, he hit his 715th home run off the Dodgers' Al Downing and took the record ("Watch: Hank Aaron"). He married Billye Williams, the widow of Rev. Samuel W. Williams (pg. 254). After his death in January 2021, his funeral was held at Friendship Baptist Church, Rev. Williams's former church, and he was laid to rest at South-View ("Hank Aaron's funeral"; pg. 254).
  * Sources: Jaffe, "Watch: Hank Aaron hits record-breaking 715th homer 40 years ago today," *Sports Illustrated*, 2014; "Hank Aaron's funeral a tribute to more than a baseball great," *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, 2021; Henderson, p. 254.

### Irene Dobbs Jackson (updates the existing card)

Henderson's entry (pg. 216) tells the library story in more detail than the current one-line card. Use this to replace it, or keep the current line.

* **Dr. Irene Dobbs Jackson (1908–1999)**, the eldest daughter of John Wesley Dobbs, taught French at Spelman College after earning her master's and doctoral degrees at the University of Toulouse in France (pg. 216). At the time, Black Atlantans could not hold a card at the main branch of the Atlanta Public Library. In March 1959, Spelman history professor Howard Zinn and Atlanta University dean Whitney Young began a federal lawsuit against the library with two plaintiffs, Jackson and Rev. Otis Moss, a Morehouse theology student (pg. 216). The library board agreed to desegregate without a court fight, and in 1959 Jackson became the first African American to receive a card from the main branch (pg. 216). Her daughter Constance recalled threatening phone calls and drive-by harassment in the days that followed (pg. 216). Her son, Maynard Jackson Jr., became Atlanta's first Black mayor (pg. 216). Her epitaph at South-View, from Kahlil Gibran, begins "You have walked among us as a spirit" (pg. 129).
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 129, 216.
