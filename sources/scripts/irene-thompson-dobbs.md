**Irene Thompson Dobbs – 1906 Script**

Good afternoon, everyone. I am Mrs. Irene Thompson Dobbs. You may already know of my husband, Mr. John Wesley Dobbs. We were married on June 6, 1906, at my sister’s house on Auburn Avenue. Rev. Henry Hugh Proctor was pastor of the First Congregational Church, where we attended services, and he conducted our marriage ceremony in front our family and friends…it was a glorious day for all of us.

When we were first got married, Wes and I lived with my sister. And that’s where we were during the Atlanta Race Massacre of 1906. We were newlyweds and enjoying what should have been a wonderful start to our life together, but I’ll never forget those ugly days in September when the world seemed to just be going crazy…all around us. When we first heard about what was happening, I was terrified. All of us were. We bolted our doors and windows and prepared for the worst. We kept hearing stories about the terrible things that had happened when the mob started attacking black people on the streets downtown.

Wes was working as a mail clerk for the railroad. He had a colt revolver that came with his job. He spent several nights awake and guarding our house. I can still see him crouched behind the front door arranging his ammunition so that he would be ready if any of the white mob tried to enter our home. It seemed as if everyone was blaming the race mingling in the Decatur Street dives for causing the violence. But the white men and boys that were rioting in the streets, they went after middle -class black neighborhoods, too. Wes thought they didn’t like competing with black folks for the good jobs in Atlanta.

On Sunday, Rev. Proctor had preached about the “black degenerates and idlers in the barrooms and gambling dens on Decatur Street.” He wanted to clean out those places. He said, “they were the hotbed of criminality and deviltry among some of our men.” But Wes thought it was wrong to place the blame solely on those black men and ignore the real race problems we were having.

We stayed in the house for days, and it was quite a while before I felt safe to be out on the streets again. The mob violence, the race hatred, the threat to our home and loved ones, the senseless deaths, all of it angered Wes, and he held onto that anger for the rest of his life.

The events of those horrible nights in 1906 played a big part in his determination to become an advocate for voting rights and social justice. <mark>Wes believed that voting was the key to solving the race problem, so in 1936 Wes set as his goal to register 10,000 Black voters and he did it! He also founded the Atlanta Civic and Political League to keep voter registration on people’s minds. Wes served as the Grand Master of the Prince Hall Masons and traveled all over Georgia speaking and encouraging everyone to register to vote … and to vote. His message still rings true today.</mark>

Once we were able to buy our own home, we moved just a few blocks away to 540 Houston Street. And that’s where we raised our family. I’m going to brag a bit now…Wes and I are the proud parents of six beautiful, intelligent, and talented daughters. All six graduated from Spelman College with honors. They’re all married now, with careers and children of their own. <mark>They all are playing significant roles in the fight for equity in their own communities.</mark> *(lean forward with your hand up to you face like you are telling a secret)* <mark>I hear that our first grandson, Maynard, was elected the first African American mayor of Atlanta after I passed. What a proud moment for the entire family and the city of Atlanta. I can’t help but think that the work Wes did laid the groundwork for Maynard’s election to happen.</mark>

Despite those early memories of bloodshed and brutality that still haunt me, Wes and I couldn’t have loved an area more than we loved Auburn Avenue, our “Sweet Auburn,” as we called it. It was where we met, and where we were married. And the history of that street will always be a tribute to black people and our contributions to this city. Some people refer to Wes as the unofficial “Mayor of Auburn Avenue,” and we’re proud of his work to achieve racial equality in segregated Atlanta.

Yes, Auburn Avenue was tangible evidence of the progress of our race—from the substantial homes, thriving businesses, churches, and community organizations—to the social life that revolved around the restaurants and the night clubs. In 1956, *Fortune* magazine described Auburn Avenue as “the richest Negro Street in the world.” That’s true, there was nowhere else like Auburn Avenue.

Some of our history is hard to hear about, and sometimes even harder to tell. But the 1906 Atlanta Race Massacre changed us. It changed everything for us in this city. And everyone needs to know that history. Thank you for coming, everyone. Have a good day, and God bless….
