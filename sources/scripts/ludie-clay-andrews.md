**Ludie Andrews - Script**

*Setting: Mrs. Andrews is dressed in a nurse’s uniform, or white blouse and black skirt, holding a clipboard with a pen in the other hand. She is doing an inventory of medications; picking up a medicine bottle, reading the label, checking it off the list….As the tour group gathers, she looks up and puts away the clipboard.*

Good Afternoon and thank you for visiting the Grady Municipal Training School for Colored Nurses. I am Superintendent Ludie Clay Andrews, and we prepare young black women for careers in nursing. Grady Hospital opened in 1892, and it was segregated like every public place in Atlanta. They had to hire black nurses, male and female, to take care of the black patients in the segregated wing of the hospital. And when Grady opened a training program for white nurses in 1898, they of course would not admit black students.

Remember now, white nurses didn’t take care of black patients at Grady, and after a while—too long, in my estimation--they realized that they needed to train black nurses too. So, Grady hired me in 1914 to supervise their black nurses and to start a training program. We held our classes at Spelman, my alma mater, so I was already familiar with the facilities and the campus. It took three years, but we received our school charter and certification in 1917. You see? (*Proudly shows the Charter, framed and sitting on the table*). Our first class, a total of six black nursing students, graduated in 1920. Oh, that was a proud day for us, but now we’re fighting with the certification board to offer our nurses the same examination given to white nurses, so that we too can be Registered Nurses. Our curriculum is the same and our students are just as qualified. If they would just test us, they would see*.* Can you believe that they offered to give ME the test, but not the other black nurses…just to keep me quiet? You may call me stubborn, but I won’t back down until every nurse, regardless of race, has the same professional opportunities!

Nursing is a noble profession. I knew it was my calling early in life. You see, when I was only 8 years old there was a fire at my family home when my mother and father were away at church. I smelled the smoke and got my older brother out safely, but my baby sister and brother didn’t survive the fire. If I had been a nurse, I believe I could have saved them.

I was born in Milledgeville, GA in 1872. I married at age 16, but my husband never stood in the way of my career. In 1906, after completing a three-year program at Spelman Seminary, I graduated with my degree in nursing. It was a rigorous program with practical experience at the MacVicar Hospital which was located right on campus. After graduation, I was hired as the superintendent of Atlanta’s Lula Grove Hospital and Training School for Colored Nurses and Patients. Lula Grove merged with Emory Hospital, and that was when I was hired by Grady.

After a long struggle and some legal wrangling, we succeeded in opening the Georgia Registered Nurse examination to all graduates of accredited nursing schools! I became Georgia’s first black registered nurse, and other black nurses quickly followed me in achieving that professional status. But you know, Grady still kept two separate programs for black and white nurses until it desegregated in 1964. After my time at Grady, I moved to Morehouse as Superintendent of the Fair Haven Infirmary and eventually returned to my beloved Spelman as superintendent of McVicar Hospital. I retired in 1948, but I stayed busy with my community work. I was an original board member of Atlanta’s Urban League, a life-long member of the Phyllis Wheatley YWCA and a board member for the colored branch of the Atlanta Tuberculosis Association.

As a child, I had survived when my parents’ house caught fire, but in 1969, when my own home burned, I was too badly injured to recover. I feel so honored that my life is remembered by the Conclave of Grady Graduate Nurses and now by the Historic South-View Preservation Foundation. Why I was even honored as a Georgia Woman of Achievement! It warms my heart to think that my life’s work is still recognized. But none of that would have happened without a lot of effort, and I must get back to work now. The future of nursing is depending upon me! *(turns back to the clipboard and becomes engrossed in writing her notes.)*
