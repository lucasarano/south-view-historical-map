*Setting: Mr. Dobbs, dressed in a suit and tie or a collared shirt with a smoking jacket and suit pants and hard shoes. He is at his home 540 Houston Street, reminiscing about an Emancipation Day speech that he made . He is looking away from the crowd speaking as though he is addressing a crowd as they group approaches.…. Say some or all this portion of the speach until the group is in front of him.*

“ ‘Over the doorway of the nation's Supreme Court Building in Washington, D. C. are engraved four words, ‘Equal Justice Under Law’. This beautiful American ideal is what the Negroes want to see operative and effective from the Atlantic to the Pacific and from the Great Lakes to the Gulf - nothing more or less. They want equal rights and protection…”

Well……. You caught me reminiscing of a time when I was Emancipation Day Speaker for ‘Wings over Jordan', a radio program heard every Sunday morning over the CBS. It gave our people an opportunity to hear perspectives that were not usually broadcast over the airways. It was one of the many honors of my life.

In my estimation, voting was the path to racial equality. I was a proponent of voting rights throughout my adult life. 10,000 Negro voters were registered through the efforts of the Atlanta Civic and Political League that I founded in 1936 and those efforts continued with the Atlanta Negro Voters League that Attorney A. T. Walton and I founded in 1946.

You see, I was born in 1882 at the foot of Kennesaw Mountain. My parents separated when I was 2 and left me and my sister, who was 4, with my grandparents. My grandparents had 14 children of their own, with my father, Will, being the oldest. It was a huge family, and they gave us all that they could, but it was barely enough to survive. When I was 9, my mother moved us, with her, to Savannah. We attended school regularly and I was enrolled in the second grade. Initially, I recall it being difficult for me to catch up with the class. I studied hard and was promoted at the end of that year. After that first year, I was the leader of every subsequent class. No one studied harder than I did.

To help my mother with expenses I found employment after school as a shoeshine boy and then as a newspaper carrier for the Savannah Press. There was a contest to see which carrier could sell the most papers over a period of weeks. The prize was a brand-new gold watch. Well, that was the first watch that I ever owned. I worked for the rest of my life…field hand, drug store clerk, paper carrier, mail clerk, activist…. From that point on I always had at least one job.

I finished the 5<sup>th</sup> grade in Savannah and then followed my mother to Atlanta. Rev. E. J. Fisher, pastor of Mt Olive Baptist Church in Atlanta, took me to the Atlanta Baptist College Academy and paid the tuition for my first year. Thereafter, I was awarded scholarships and finished the academy in 4 years. After the academy I enrolled in Morehouse College, but during my freshman year, my mother fell ill, and I had to drop out. I never graduated from college, but that did not stop me from becoming educated. I was a voracious reader. If I wasn’t working, I was studying.

In 1903, I passed the civil service exam and was certified for appointment as a railway mail clerk for the US Post Office. My route was from Atlanta to Nashville. I held that position for 32 years, retiring at the highest level, as Clerk in Charge of an all-white crew. That was an unusual situation for the times, but I never experienced any friction with a crew member that we were not able to resolve satisfactorily to all. We worked together beautifully.

In 1906, I married the love of my life….Irene Ophelia Thompson. She was from Columbus, MS and was the most beautiful girl I had ever seen. She and I raised 6 talented, spirited daughters who all graduated from Spelman College with honors - two of them as valedictorian of their classes. I required them to take 9 years of piano and forbad them to patronize segregated theatres. All of them would go on to achieve advanced degrees and positively impact the communities where they lived. Why my second to the youngest, Mattiwilda, was a world renown opera singer! My wife and I raised them to be the ethical, moral, community-oriented activists and I proclaim that we succeeded with each one.

In 1911, I enthusiastically joined the Prince Hall Masons. After only three years I was named Grand Hall Officer, a position I held for 10 years. Subsequently, I was made secretary-treasurer of the Masonic Relief Association, a position that paid more than my railway clerk job. In 1932, I was elected Grand Master - head of the order in the state of Georgia a role that I filled until my death. My preparation for public life through reading and studying stood me in good stead. I traveled the state speaking to Masons about political activism and registering to vote. I often took my wife and two younger daughters with me to expose them to different communities in Georgia. Largely because of our voter registration efforts, Mayor Hartsfield appointed 8 Black Police Officers in 1948. They could only patrol the Negro areas and could not arrest White people, but it was a first step toward integrating the Atlanta Police Force. The following year the Mayor installed streetlights down Auburn Avenue. My “Sweet Auburn”. The business district for everything Black Atlanta. Except for our family home on Houston Street, I couldn’t have loved an area more. It was tangible evidence of the progress of the race, from the financial institutions, architectural organizations, retail establishments to the social engagement centers. In fact, one could find the offices for South-View Cemetery in the Odd Fellows Building on Auburn Avenue until just before I passed.

Sadly, on August 21, 1961, I suffered a stroke and passed 9 days later, leaving my loving family behind in this world. Although I was a lifelong member of First Congregational Church, my funeral was held at Big Bethel AME because it was located on Sweet Auburn. Rev. Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. spoke at my funeral and Thurgood Marshall served as one of my pallbearers. As Grand Master of the Prince Hall Masons, this beautiful monument was erected at my gravesite.

They tell me that my first grandson, Maynard Jackson, became the first African American mayor of Atlanta, and renamed Houston Ave to John Wesley Dobbs Ave. in my memory!

I could talk about old Atlanta all afternoon, but I will let you go on your way. Remember the power of the vote! Register and Vote every time there is an election!

.
