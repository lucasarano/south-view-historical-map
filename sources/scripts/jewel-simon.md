**Jewel Simon**

Art is an expression. The scene, the color tones, the contrast, the style …all open to interpretation from one’s experiences and emotions. When you look at this painting, what do you see? Art patrons use limited personal views to determine whether a piece is worthy of acclaim or recognition. Thank goodness I never let others define the value of MY work.   I created it…on my own terms.

I am Jewel Simon, born in Houston, TX, in 1911, I graduated as valedictorian from high school at the age of 15,,,then summa cum laude from Atlanta University with a degree in mathematics. My first job was teaching high school math back in Houston. One of my students admired my sketches and asked if I would donate them to raise money for band uniforms. Surprised at how quickly my sketches sold out, I began to focus on my art. I was tutored by a local artist to hone my skills, and I entered a few segregated art shows to display my pieces.

Then I met and married the love of my life, Edward Simon, who sold insurance for the Atlanta Life Company. After a few years, Edward was promoted to a position at the home office in Atlanta. Here is where we established roots and raised our two children. I loved being a wife and mother…PTA meetings, Jack & Jill events, and Sunday school. Art was only a “pleasant hobby”. Family was my top priority.

Oh, I still found time to paint and submit some works for exhibition. Opportunities for black artists to participate in non-segregated art shows in the South were limited. I decided to enter juried shows, and in 1943 my work was accepted into Atlanta University’s national art exhibit, winning several prizes! I was thrilled to study painting with prominent artist Hale Woodruff and sculpture with Alice Dunbar. Those relationships-built confidence and exposure in Atlanta’s art world as I established myself as a recognizable talent.  But how could my art be assessed fairly in a system that didn’t value me as a person?

I submitted pictures of my sculptures to the New York Metropolitan Museum of Art’s competition, *Sculpture1951*. Although my work was not selected for the show, the Met Museum cataloged me as… an artist! After that I began identifying myself as a serious artist. 

In 1952, I entered a piece into the *Southeastern Annual Art Exhibition* organized by the Atlanta Arts Association. I submitted a watercolor entitled *City Slums* under the “race paintings” genre. Typically, race paintings were caricatures of black life created by white artists, perpetuating black stereotypes. My painting depicted a city skyline towering over a group of unassuming row houses –  a familiar urban landscape. My entry not only challenged the norms of the contest, but reflected how race impacts the development of our cities.

While I did not win a prize for *City Slums, *I received an honorable mention in the Atlanta Journal-Constitution as well as an invitation to the show’s opening reception tea at the High Museum. Edward and I arrived in formal attire…Ed in a sharp tux and I in a chiffon gown. The moment we stepped inside the High Museum, we were stopped… questioned. I ducked through the stiff crowd and stood by my painting on display. I had to show them why I was there!

I challenged the gatekeepers of the art industry and applied to the Atlanta School of Art. Of course, my application was denied. Undeterred, I applied again the next year, and the following year, and the year after that.…after ten years, I was finally accepted. In 1967, I became the first African American to graduate from the Atlanta School of Art with a Bachelor of Fine Arts.

With Edward rising in the ranks to Chairman of the Board at The Atlanta Life Company and my children busy with their own endeavors, I focused on studying my craft and my recognition grew. During my career I received eight Purchase Prize Awards from Atlanta University, the James Weldon Johnson Award from the NAACP, and the coveted John Hope Prize. I was the co-founder of the National Conference of Artists - one of the first organizations to highlight black artists. My work has been exhibited in museums and galleries all over the world from the Apex Museum right here in Atlanta, to SoHo and Harlem, NY… all the way to the Metropolitan Museum in Tokyo, Japan. Not bad for a housewife with a little hobby, huh?

In my later years, I found a new hobby and began writing poetry. I published a collection of my poems and artwork that won the Golden Poet Award in 1985. In December of 1996 at the age of 85 my life came to an end. I was a loving wife and devoted mother who also happened to be an artist. My work doesn’t hang in the Louvre, but my painting, *City Slums* is displayed at Clark University. I’m proud that my quiet determination broke barriers and paved the way for other Black artists to be valued for their work. Believe in yourselves, know your own value and be determined to make the world around you a more beautiful place …on your own terms.
