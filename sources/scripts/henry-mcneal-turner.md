**Bishop Henry McNeal Turner**

I was born free in South Carolina,\
but freedom—true freedom—has never been a birthright in this nation.\
It is a demand.\
It is a struggle.\
It is a claim.

My name is **Henry McNeal Turner**.\
I came to Georgia in the aftermath of war, not as a bystander to history, but as a builder of it.\
As a minister of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, I planted congregations across this state, because I knew the church would be the first schoolhouse, the first meeting hall, the first political training ground for a people newly freed but not yet protected.

Across Georgia, I, alongside other freedmen, organized Black men to vote,\
and I urged Black women to understand their political power, even when denied suffrage rights,\
because emancipation without participation is only another form of control.

I witnessed a moment in Georgia when democracy briefly told the truth about itself.\
I saw formerly enslaved men elected to office.\
I helped draft a new state constitution.\
I stood in the Georgia Legislature in 1868 as one of the elected representatives of the people.

And then I watched Georgia retreat.

When Black citizens gained power, the state responded with fear.\
When we exercised the vote, they rewrote the law.\
When the people spoke, the legislature expelled us.

Some of us were imprisoned, attacked, forced to flee the state, or killed.

Reconstruction did not fail because Black leadership was unprepared—\
it failed because white supremacy would not surrender control.

*(Voting Rights & Women’s Rights)*

It was in that chamber, beneath the dome of the state capitol,\
that I stood and declared words that still echo beyond my lifetime:

**I shall not beg for my rights.**

I would not fawn before men who feared equality.\
I would not plead for citizenship in a state built by Black labor.\
To beg for rights already earned is to confess inferiority—and I would never do that.

Rights are not gifts.\
They are not favors granted by legislatures.\
They are inherent, or they are meaningless.

And let me be clear—this struggle was never only for Black men.\
A democracy that silences women is a democracy already broken.\
Black women sustained our churches, educated our children, financed our movements, and carried our communities when the state abandoned them.\
To deny them political voice was to deny the future itself.

The ballot is the foundation of every other freedom.\
Take it away, and law becomes a weapon.\
Deny it, and citizenship becomes theater.

When Georgia chose disenfranchisement, it did not merely expel legislators—\
it expelled justice.

*(Church Leadership, Education, and the Black Press)(*

After my removal from office, I did not retreat.\
I returned to the work I had always known—organizing my people for survival and sovereignty.

As a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, I championed Black-controlled education and institutional independence.\
I supported the AME’s educational mission in Atlanta, including Morris Brown College, founded to ensure that Black minds would not be governed by white benevolence but shaped by Black vision and authority.

I wrote for the Black press because silence is collaboration.\
My pen spoke where the law refused to listen.\
I wrote to awaken, to warn, to insist that dignity was not negotiable.

And when the promises of Reconstruction collapsed into Jim Crow,\
I spoke openly of what others feared to say:\
that a people denied justice must consider sovereignty.

*(Black Nationalism and the Question of Africa)*

I became an advocate of Black nationalism and emigration not because I abandoned America,\
but because America had abandoned us.

I believed that Black people were a nation within a nation—\
entitled to self-determination, dignity, and destiny.\
Africa was not an escape.\
It was a declaration that we would define ourselves, whether here or elsewhere.

I refused the lie that patience was virtue when injustice was policy.

*(Closing: Passing the Torch — South-View Cemetery)*

Now, I speak as a man nearing the end of his earthly labor.

I will not live to see the full harvest of what I planted.\
But I know this: the work does not end with me.

When my body is laid to rest here at **South-View Cemetery** in Atlanta,\
I will lie among those who built this city with vision and sacrifice.\
Teachers.\
Preachers.\
Organizers.\
Freedom workers.

And one day, others will join us there—\
not as the defeated,\
but as those who carried the work forward.

So I pass the torch now to you.

Guard the vote.\
Name injustice plainly.\
Build institutions that do not beg for permission to exist.

And remember me not as a man of the past,\
but as a voice that still insists—

**I shall not beg for my rights.**
