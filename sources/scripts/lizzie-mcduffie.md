*Scene - Looking away theatrically humming the Black National Anthem*

Hello! My name is Elizabeth McDuffie, but everyone calls me Lizzie. I was born in Newton County, Ga in 1881, and my family moved to Atlanta when I was 6 years old. I loved acting in school plays and reciting poetry. I won more recitation contests than I can count… Even so, in my third year of high school at Morris Brown College, I had to drop out because we didn’t have the money.

In 1906 I was a young woman working as a live in nurse maid in the Hillyer home. Around 11:00 Sunday morning, I headed out to walk little Eleanor Hillyer home from Sunday school and I ran into another maid. She asked had I heard about Sunny Boy Smith, a Western Union delivery man that we were both friends with. He had been murdered overnight! I didn’t know what to say or what to think. When I got back to the Hillyer home I looked for the newspaper, but Mr. Hillyer had hidden it. I was afraid to go outside, even to check on my own family and friends. On Monday night there were more killings, including one of my best friends. On Tuesday morning I watched Negro families streaming past the Hillyer house on Capital Ave, walking with the belongings they could carry from South Atlanta to stay with friends or family in safer parts of the city. They were fleeing from their homes to escape the violence. Innocent men, women and children, hunted and fearful for their lives! That scene stayed with me for the rest of my life.

Several years later I landed a job in the home of Mr. and Mrs. Edward Inman where I worked for 23 years. I learned so much at Inman’s that helped me excel in my next job. To be only one generation out of slavery, my life was more amazing than I could have even dreamed! I was an advocate for racial equity, and a political spokesperson before it was all said and done. Let me tell you how it all happened!

One evening, I attended a party where I met a handsome barber named Irvin McDuffie. At the end of the party, he approached me, bowed and said, “I have been favorably impressed with you. I would like to call.” That sounds so formal now! Well, he did “call”… and two years later we were married. It was love at first sight.

Irvin, or Mac as we called him, and millionaire Alonzo Herndon had a 16-chair barber shop that served white patrons. One day, Mac was shaving a customer who asked, “How would you like a change of jobs, McDuffie? There is a fellow in Warm Springs who is looking for a valet.” A week later Mac started working for Mr. Franklin Delano Roosevelt.

For 6 years I saw the Roosevelts through my husband’s eyes and I liked what I saw. Mac was there before Mr. Roosevelt was elected Governor of NY and saw him with everyone from Kings to his grandchildren. No one knows a man better than his valet. The two of them would romp and joke all the way to Pine Mountain in a Model T Ford.

In 1933, I accepted a job as the third-floor maid at the White House, in Washington DC. As White House maid and wife of the President’s valet, I got to know the Roosevelts and they knew me. Mac and I received lots of letters from Negroes who wanted us to talk to the President for them. We shared information with him about discrimination and unfair arrests. He intervened behind the scenes when he could. I was able to help NAACP’s Walter White meet with the President on important issues of the time. Mary McLeod Bethune and many others contacted me to help them

meet with to the President….. I told Mr. Roosevelt that I was going to serve as his SASOCPA - self-appointed secretary on colored people’s affairs. We both laughed.

As Mr. Roosevelt’s campaign for re-election began, some friends asked me if I could be released from the White House to speak to their group about the election. I wasn’t at all sure that this could be done. No other staff member did it. I was so surprised that morning in 1936 when the President sent for me. He said, “Lizzie, I’m going to let you campaign for me. You can meet the public and you can talk. Just tell them what we are trying to do.” I had memorized my speech, but when I faced that first overflow crowd in St. Louis, I stopped the speech right in the middle. People wanted to know the little stuff, … What was it like to live in the White House? What sort of people the Roosevelts really are? Could Mr. Roosevelt walk at all? I spoke to crowds in Ohio, Illinois, Indiana and Pennsylvania. Upon my return from campaigning, I would meet with the President and tell him the parts that I thought would interest him. I always included that we were going to win…and we did!

Because of my campaigning, in 1940, the Afro-American magazine named me as one of their 8 Women of the Year! I forgot to tell you about my audition for the part of Mammy in “Gone with the Win” …. but I’m talking too much.

All combined, Mac and I served Mr. Roosevelt from1927 until he passed on April 12, 1945. I was at the Little White House when it happened, and I rode on the train when his body was taken back to Washington. I watched the crowds lining the tracks with tears in their eyes as an era came to an end. Mac died a year later. In 1966, I was buried beside Mac, here at South-View.

Throughout my life I never forgot Atlanta in 1906….the fear in my heart ….and on the faces of the Negros leaving their homes….the mobs of white men killing anyone they could grab! That memory is why I did my best to contribute to the progress of my race. I am proud to have been in the White House and able to take our concerns to the President and to ask for his help. I hope that I did my part so that nothing like 1906 will ever happen again. Share your special talents so that the promises of America can reach everyone!
