**John Wesley Edward Bowen, Sr.** 

Good afternoon, dear brethren! I am thrilled for the opportunity to speak with you this afternoon, but I must admit that I am deeply troubled. You see, our race has shown tremendous progress throughout the years…first black president, first black woman of the Supreme Court, a black president of an Ivy League school, numerous awards and accolades in sports and entertainment. Did you know nine Black CEOs are leading Fortune 500 companies today? And yet, it doesn’t seem that we have gained much respect or influence in the eyes of our nation. It appears we are not going forward ….but backward.

My name is John Wesley Edward Bowen, Sr. I was born enslaved in New Orleans, Louisiana, in 1855. Father was a carpenter and worked to purchase our family’s freedom and later served in the Union Army during the Civil War.

I was 13 by the end of the war and eager to start my education at the Union Normal School in New Orleans. I continued at New Orleans University, earning a Bachelor of Arts degree in 1878. I spent a few years teaching Latin, Greek, and mathematics at Tennessee College before I started my studies at Boston University. I completed a Bachelor of Sacred Theology degree at Boston University in 1885 and was voted by my classmates to speak at the commencement ceremony. I immediately began work the following year towards a Ph.D. in historical theology. I became the first African American to earn a PhD at Boston University in 1887 - the second African American to earn that degree in the United States.

Gifted in preaching, I pastored several congregations in the Methodist church and made it my personal mission to advocate for the election of black bishops. I taught briefly at Morgan College and Howard University before moving to Atlanta in 1893 to teach historical theology at Gammon Theological Seminary, becoming the first full-time black professor.

My wife, Ariel, who helped me raise four beautiful children, taught music at Clark College.

On “Negro Day” during the 1895 Atlanta Cotton States' Exposition, I gave my most well-known speech entitled "An Appeal to the King". My address highlighted the idea that racism is not just a “negro” issue:

*“Our ears have become familiar with the so-called race problem, which has been popularly interpreted to mean the Negro race problem. A truer and larger conception of the subject would speak of the human race problem.”*

Not too much difference from the current situation, huh?

In January 1904, I launched a monthly literary magazine, *The Voice of the Negro, *along with journalist Jesse Max Barber. The Voice covered the political and race relations in the South, reaching at least 15,000 subscribers. It was the first magazine publication in the South edited by Blacks. Unfortunately, this same year, my sweet Ariel passed away and is buried here with me.

To get through that difficult time I took on additional responsibilities to distract me from my loss. I was appointed president of Gammon in 1906. However, not long after my promotion, I was faced with one of the most challenging events of my life. In September 1906, during the Atlanta Race Massacre, I opened the school as a shelter for Brownsville blacks who had nowhere else to go to escape the violence. As the white mob closed in on our building with the imminent threat of death, I contacted the police for help. Instead of protection, I was severely beaten and arrested. Thank God I survived!

I remained at Gammon for nearly 40 years until my retirement in 1932. My son, John Wesley Edward, Jr., who grew up on Gammon’s campus, earned a master's degree at Harvard and became a bishop in the Methodist Church.

I died July 20, 1933, survived by my second wife, Irene, and my four children.

I preached and taught about equality throughout my life, and I am delighted by the strides we have made as a race towards equality since my time. Though there are days (especially recently) when it feels like little progress has been made, I have to be realistic.

For *“there is no such thing as perfect equality of individuals or races. This is a figment…the true or native equality of men as stated by the great Jefferson…is that all men are natively and equally endowed with the essentials of humanity and divinity. And because of this, all should be permitted to develop their endowments for the good \[of\] society.”*

If humanity can accept and adopt this idea, then I am hopeful that we can keep working toward a shared equality.
