**Dinah Watts Pace – Script**

**Scene - *Sweeping and cleaning up an area…maybe humming a tune***

Good day, ladies and gentlemen. Welcome to South-View. My family has been part of South-View since its very beginning. Our family lot is just over there. But that’s not where our history with this place started. My name is Dinah Watts Pace, and my brother Albert Watts was one of the founders, and our brother Lewis was the first secretary of the cemetery corporation. Down through the years, there’s always been a Watts family member or two working at South-View. Our legacy here, caring for families and maintaining these hallowed grounds has been a source of family pride.

My brothers and I were born slaves in Athens, Georgia, but we landed in Atlanta in the years after the Civil War. We lived in Summerhill, and since there was no church nearby, I gathered with the other children in the neighborhood, and we started out own Sunday school. We met each week and learned about the Bible. In time we had our church! It was called Pleasant Grove Baptist Church. Later the church was renamed Reed Street Baptist Church, and it exists to this day as Paradise Missionary Baptist Church.

I graduated from the Normal School at Atlanta University in 1883. Then I went to Covington, Georgia, to teach school. It was just a two-room schoolhouse. We didn’t have any real furniture, or any money to buy it with, so we just made do with dry goods boxes that we knocked together to make desks and seats. I was only supposed to stay in Covington for a few months, but I started visiting the students at home and talking with their families. I found that the mothers in the community needed my help and encouragement as well.

That next year, I took charge of two little orphans. As word spread about my “open house” policy for homeless children, I soon had four, then five children in my care. The youngest ones went with me to school each day. The older ones worked on neighboring farms, and I tried to teach them at night. I soon gave up my teaching job and spent my time raising funds and working with the orphans.

I met Mr. Pace in Covington. He was a blacksmith. He was twenty years older than me, and a widower. We married on June 21, 1888. Mr. Pace not only helped with the children, but he also made some of the things we needed. Many husbands would not have wanted a working wife, but Mr. Pace allowed me to be me and supported my dreams for the children and the school. He was a wonderful man!

My fundraising brought in more donations, and in the fall of 1890, I applied to be incorporated as the Covington Colored Orphans Home. Mrs. A. C. Reed from Vermont sent \$1000! Enough to build a real orphanage—the building was two and a half stories tall with 10 rooms! In 1903, we became the Reed Home and Industrial School to honor our most generous benefactor. As more children arrived, we needed more teachers, and like me, they all came from Atlanta University. My niece Annie May Watts was one of our teachers. I was not blessed with children of my own, but Annie May was just like a daughter….following in my footsteps.

The children and I grew vegetables on a two-acre garden around the home. I bought a 100-acre farm some distance away, and we worked that too. My goal was to give the children a practical—as well as an intellectual—education. We had a motto, “We Are Inching Along,” and that was also our school song. Despite the obstacles in our way, no matter how much we struggled, just like the inch worm, we could still make progress, if we just kept “inching along.”

Our home became a model for social services to children, and W.E.B. DuBois invited me to participate in his conferences and speak about the work I was doing. Many people, like the women of Wellesley College, supported my work through their donations of money, food and clothing for the children. My brother Lewis gave most of his monthly salary as a Pullman Porter to help support the orphanage and he always brough us books from the places along his routes.

We continued to expand the orphanage and added more buildings to the property, but we did have our share of challenges. We survived through the death of Mr. Pace, we survived despite the fire that destroyed the girls’ dormitory, we survived the year the boll-weevil destroyed all our crops, and we were still “inching along” at the onset of the Great Depression. One day, I suffered a dizzy spell. I stepped too close to the fireplace, and my skirt caught fire. They brought me to the hospital in Atlanta, but my burns were too severe.

My niece Annie May and the other teachers kept striving after my death, but with the increasing financial debts, the orphanage closed a few years later. We had sheltered more than 700 children over the years, and those children went on to better lives because of the work we did. Every little step we took turned out to be a fraction of a bigger step toward bettering the lives of the children and the community. I hear they even named a street in Covington in my honor!

My death came on January 25, 1933, but you won’t find that date on my headstone. In fact, there are no dates on any of the headstones in our Watts family lot. But I’ve already kept you long enough; I’ll save that story for another day. Enjoy the rest of your visit…there’s a lot more history for you to hear about in this cemetery.
