## Atlanta's First Black Police Officers (1948)

Sources:
1. "Tracing the Atlanta 8 through Sweet Auburn." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/tracing-the-atlanta-8-through-sweet-auburn/.
2. "Black in Blue: Atlanta's First African American Police Officers." *Atlanta*, https://www.atlantamagazine.com/great-reads/black-blue-atlantas-first-african-american-police-officers-vanguards-civil-rights-movement/.
3. "Willard L. Strickland Papers." Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History, finding aid, https://aafa.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/25.
4. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 177, 242–43.

South-View Residents Involved
* Willard L. Strickland – One of the eight officers (pg. 242–43).
* Rev. William Holmes Borders – Helped lead the push to desegregate the Atlanta Police Department in the 1940s (pg. 177).

Historical Notes
* In 1948, eight Black men joined the Atlanta Police Department: Henry Hooks, Claude Dixon, Ernest H. Lyons, Robert McKibbens, Willard Strickland, Willie T. Elkins, Johnnie P. Jones, and John Sanders. (Sources 1, 2)
* About four hundred people turned out to watch their first patrol on April 3, 1948. (Source 1)
* They were not allowed in police headquarters and worked out of a precinct in the basement of the Butler Street YMCA. (Sources 1, 4; pg. 242)
* They could not wear their uniforms to and from work, could not drive squad cars, and patrolled Black neighborhoods on foot. (pg. 242)
* If they stopped a white suspect, they had to call a white officer, and they were not allowed to arrest white people until 1962. (Source 1)

Narrative for website

Atlanta hired its first Black police officers in 1948, after pressure from Black civic leaders, including Rev. William Holmes Borders of Wheat Street Baptist Church (pg. 177). Eight men joined the force that spring: Henry Hooks, Claude Dixon, Ernest H. Lyons, Robert McKibbens, Willard Strickland, Willie T. Elkins, Johnnie P. Jones, and John Sanders ("Tracing the Atlanta 8"). About four hundred people turned out to watch their first patrol on April 3, 1948 ("Tracing the Atlanta 8"). The eight were not allowed in police headquarters, so they worked out of the basement of the Butler Street YMCA. They could not wear their uniforms to and from work or drive squad cars, and they walked their beats in Black neighborhoods (pg. 242). If they stopped a white suspect, they had to call a white officer, and they could not arrest white people until 1962 ("Tracing the Atlanta 8"). Willard Strickland, one of the eight, rests at South-View (pg. 242–43).

## Southern Christian Leadership Conference Founded (1957)

Sources:
1. "1946–1966: From Civil Rights to Black Power: The Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)." *This Far by Faith*, PBS, https://www.pbs.org/thisfarbyfaith/popup/4_sclc.html.
2. "Reasons to Celebrate Black History Month: SCLC." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://www.ajc.com/lifestyles/reasons-celebrate-black-history-month-sclc/ZhIaBVoyRuhzlPiE7e91CK/.
3. "Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)." *Encyclopedia of African-American Culture and History*, via Encyclopedia.com, https://encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/southern-christian-leadership-conference-sclc.
4. "Prince Hall Masonic Temple." Explore Georgia, https://exploregeorgia.org/atlanta/history-heritage/african-american/prince-hall-masonic-temple.
5. "Southern Christian Leadership Conference." Georgia Historical Society, historical marker, https://www.georgiahistory.com/ghmi_marker_updated/southern-christian-leadership-conference/.
6. "Martin Luther King Jr. Fast Facts." CNN, via KEYT, 7 Jan. 2026, https://keyt.com/news/national-world/cnn-national/2026/01/07/martin-luther-king-jr-fast-facts/.
7. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 100, 174, 219, 220, 239, 254.

South-View Residents Involved
* John Wesley Dobbs – Led the Prince Hall Masons in building their temple on Auburn Avenue, where the SCLC opened its first headquarters (pg. 100; Source 4).
* Rev. Samuel W. Williams – Held leadership positions in the SCLC (pg. 254).
* Rev. A.D. Williams King – Served on the SCLC's board (pg. 219).
* Rev. Fred C. Bennette Jr. – Directed SCLC voter registration drives and was the first director of its Operation Breadbasket (pg. 174).
* Herman J. Russell – A major donor to the SCLC (pg. 239).

Historical Notes
* In January 1957, after the Montgomery Bus Boycott, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders, including Bayard Rustin and Ella Baker, called about sixty Black ministers and leaders to a meeting in Atlanta at Ebenezer Baptist Church. (Sources 1, 2)
* The group was first called the Southern Negro Leaders Conference on Transportation and Nonviolent Integration and took the name Southern Christian Leadership Conference later in 1957. It used nonviolent direct action to end legal and social discrimination. (Source 3)
* King was its first president. (Source 6)
* The SCLC opened its first headquarters in the Prince Hall Masonic Temple on Auburn Avenue and stayed there until it moved next door to 320 Auburn Avenue in 2007. (Sources 4, 5)
* The Prince Hall Masons built their temple on Auburn Avenue under the leadership of John Wesley Dobbs, starting in 1937. (pg. 100)

Narrative for website

In January 1957, soon after the Montgomery Bus Boycott, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders called about sixty Black ministers and activists to Atlanta to carry the boycott's nonviolent methods across the South ("This Far by Faith"). They met at Ebenezer Baptist Church, where King's father was pastor ("Reasons to Celebrate Black History Month: SCLC"; pg. 220). Later that year the group took the name Southern Christian Leadership Conference, with King as its first president, and it set out to end segregation through nonviolent direct action ("Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)"; "Martin Luther King Jr. Fast Facts"). Its first headquarters was on Auburn Avenue in the Prince Hall Masonic Temple, which the Masons had built under the leadership of John Wesley Dobbs (pg. 100; "Prince Hall Masonic Temple"). Several South-View residents helped carry its work: Rev. Samuel W. Williams held leadership roles in the SCLC, Rev. A.D. King served on its board, Rev. Fred C. Bennette Jr. directed its voter registration drives and was the first director of its Operation Breadbasket, and Herman J. Russell was a major donor (pg. 174, 219, 239, 254).

## Atlanta Public Library Desegregated (1959)

Sources:
1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 187, 216, 228, 258.
2. "The Woman Who Desegregated Atlanta's Library System." The Atlanta 100, 20 Feb. 2018, https://theatlanta100.com/history/2018/02/20/desegregation-of-atlantas-library-system/15230.
3. "Case Study: Atlanta." *History of US Public Libraries*, Digital Public Library of America, https://dp.la/exhibitions/history-us-public-libraries/segregated-libraries/case-study-atlanta.
4. "The Woman Behind a Family of History Makers: The Life of Dr. Irene Dobbs Jackson." Westside Future Fund, 15 Mar. 2023, https://westsidefuturefund.org/?p=6726.

South-View Residents Involved
* Dr. Irene Dobbs Jackson – One of two plaintiffs in the 1959 lawsuit; the first African American to receive a card from the main branch (pg. 216).
* Alice Dugged Cary – In 1921, the first librarian of the Auburn Avenue Branch, the library's branch for Black Atlantans (pg. 187).
* Annie L. McPheeters – The first professionally trained Black librarian in the Atlanta Public Library system; managed the Auburn Branch and, from 1949, the West Hunter Branch (pg. 228).
* Ella Gaines Yates – In 1976, the first African American director of the Atlanta-Fulton Public Library system (pg. 258).

Historical Notes
* Before 1959, Black Atlantans could read at the main library only in its basement. (Source 2)
* In March 1959, Spelman history professor Howard Zinn and Atlanta University dean Whitney Young began a federal lawsuit against the library with two plaintiffs, Irene Dobbs Jackson and Rev. Otis Moss, a Morehouse theology student. (pg. 216)
* The library board agreed to desegregate without a court fight. Jackson received a card from the main branch, and Spelman music professor Earl A. Sanders received his at the same time. (pg. 216)
* Jackson's daughter Constance recalled threatening phone calls and drive-by harassment in the days that followed. (pg. 216)
* The Atlanta–Fulton Public Library System formally desegregated in 1959. (Source 3)

Narrative for website

For decades, Black Atlantans could not borrow books from the city's main library and could read there only in the basement ("The Woman Who Desegregated Atlanta's Library System"). They were served instead at separate branches, such as the Auburn Avenue Branch, whose first librarian in 1921 was Alice Dugged Cary, and the West Hunter Branch, managed from 1949 by Annie L. McPheeters (pg. 187, 228). In March 1959, Spelman history professor Howard Zinn and Atlanta University dean Whitney Young began a federal lawsuit against the library with two plaintiffs, Spelman professor Dr. Irene Dobbs Jackson and Rev. Otis Moss, a Morehouse theology student (pg. 216). Rather than fight in court, the library board agreed to desegregate. Jackson became the first African American to receive a card from the main branch, and music professor Earl A. Sanders received his at the same time (pg. 216). Jackson's daughter Constance later recalled threatening phone calls and drive-by harassment in the days that followed (pg. 216). In 1976, Ella Gaines Yates became the first African American director of the Atlanta-Fulton Public Library system (pg. 258). Jackson, Cary, McPheeters, and Yates all rest at South-View.

## Atlanta Schools and UGA Desegregated (1961)

Sources:
1. "NAACP and School Desegregation." Robert W. Woodruff Library, Emory University, https://scholarblogs.emory.edu/woodruff/fyi/naacp-and-school-desegregation.
2. "Photos: How Atlanta Public Schools Integrated in 1961." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://ajc.com/education/photos-how-atlanta-public-schools-integrated-in-1961/D7DZPW7P2VCT5CLWYYC5PAPNPI.
3. "Charlayne Hunter-Gault." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/people/charlayne-hunter-gault-b-1942/.
4. "Hamilton Holmes." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/education/hamilton-holmes-1941-1995/.
5. "Ernest Vandiver: Hunter-Holmes Controversy." WSB-TV newsfilm, record contributed by the *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://lib-umbra-prd-app-01.oit.umn.edu/catalog/e96cf1eeea4b5a3f7304abb6dbf423eb71c14709.
6. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 177, 211.

South-View Residents Involved
* Jesse Hill Jr. – Met Hamilton Holmes and Charlayne Hunter in 1959 and helped them look for a white Georgia college to desegregate (Source 4).
* Dr. Horace Mann Bond – Did research for the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund in *Brown v. Board of Education* (pg. 177).

Historical Notes
* After a two-year legal fight by the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund and Atlanta lawyers Donald Hollowell, Vernon Jordan, and Horace Ward, federal judge William Bootle ruled on January 6, 1961, that Hamilton Holmes and Charlayne Hunter must be admitted to the University of Georgia. (Source 3)
* Holmes and Hunter arrived to register on January 9, 1961, and were met with taunts and racial slurs. (Source 3)
* Two days later, after a basketball game, a crowd gathered outside Hunter's dormitory and smashed windows with bottles and bricks. The university suspended both students, saying it was for their safety, and they returned to class days later under a new court order. (Source 5)
* The NAACP filed its first lawsuit against the Atlanta Public Schools, *Calhoun v. Latimer*, in 1958. (Source 1)
* On August 30, 1961, nine Black students entered four all-white Atlanta high schools: Brown, Grady, Murphy, and Northside. (Sources 1, 2)
* The nine, remembered as the Atlanta Nine, were Madelyn Nix and Thomas Welch (Brown); Willie Jean Black, Arthur Simmons, and Donita Gaines (Northside); Lawrence Jefferson and Mary McMullen (Grady); and Martha Ann Holmes and Rosalyn Walton (Murphy). (Source 2)

Narrative for website

Seven years after the Supreme Court's 1954 ruling in *Brown v. Board of Education*, Georgia's schools and colleges were still segregated. Dr. Horace Mann Bond, who later led Atlanta University's school of education, had helped research the *Brown* case for the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund (pg. 177). In 1961, the barriers began to fall. After a two-year legal fight by the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund and Atlanta lawyers Donald Hollowell, Vernon Jordan, and Horace Ward, federal judge William Bootle ordered the University of Georgia on January 6 to admit Hamilton Holmes and Charlayne Hunter ("Charlayne Hunter-Gault"). Jesse Hill Jr. had met the two Turner High School graduates in 1959 and helped them look for a white Georgia college to desegregate ("Hamilton Holmes"). When they arrived to register on January 9, they were met with taunts and slurs. Two days later, after a basketball game, a crowd gathered outside Hunter's dormitory and smashed windows, and the university suspended both students until a new court order returned them to class ("Charlayne Hunter-Gault"; "Ernest Vandiver"). That August, after an NAACP lawsuit first filed in 1958, nine Black students entered four all-white Atlanta high schools, Brown, Grady, Murphy, and Northside, on August 30, 1961 ("NAACP and School Desegregation"). Remembered as the Atlanta Nine, they were Madelyn Nix, Thomas Welch, Willie Jean Black, Arthur Simmons, Donita Gaines, Lawrence Jefferson, Mary McMullen, Martha Ann Holmes, and Rosalyn Walton ("Photos: How Atlanta Public Schools Integrated"). Hill and Bond rest at South-View.

## Peyton Road Wall (1962 – 1963)

Sources:
1. "Atlanta's Berlin Wall." Atlanta History Center, https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/atlantas-berlin-wall/.
2. "Atlanta's 'Berlin Wall.'" *Atlanta*, https://www.atlantamagazine.com/civilrights/atlantas-berlin-wall/.
3. Bayor, Ronald H. "Ivan Allen Jr. Biography: Peyton Road Wall." Ivan Allen Jr. Digital Collection, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://ivanallen.iac.gatech.edu/biography/peyton-road-wall.
4. "Civil Rights Giant and Activist Clinton E. Warner, Jr., MD Succumbs at Age 87." *The Atlanta Inquirer*, https://atlinq.com/civil-rights-giant-and-activist-clinton-e-warner-jr-md-succumbs-at-age-87/.

South-View Residents Involved
* None known. (See the notes at the end.)

Historical Notes
* In December 1962, Dr. Clinton Warner, a Black surgeon, bought a home on twenty acres in the Peyton Forest section of Cascade Heights in southwest Atlanta, a white neighborhood. (Sources 1, 2)
* White homeowners asked the city to block the roads into their neighborhood. On December 17, the Board of Aldermen approved the plan, and Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. had barricades built across Peyton Road and Harlan Road. (Sources 1, 2)
* Black Atlantans called the barricade "Atlanta's Berlin Wall." (Source 2)
* Attorney Donald Hollowell sued in Fulton County Superior Court. On March 1, 1963, Judge George P. Whitman ruled the barricades unconstitutional, and the mayor had them taken down within minutes. (Sources 2, 4)
* Georgia Tech's Ivan Allen biography describes the wall as part of an effort to keep the neighborhood white. (Source 3)

Narrative for website

In December 1962, Dr. Clinton Warner, a Black surgeon, bought a home in Peyton Forest, a white section of Cascade Heights in southwest Atlanta ("Atlanta's Berlin Wall"). White homeowners asked the city to keep more Black families out, and on December 17 the Board of Aldermen approved their plan. Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. then had barricades built across Peyton Road and Harlan Road, cutting the white streets off from the Black neighborhood next to them ("Atlanta's Berlin Wall"; "Atlanta's 'Berlin Wall'"). Black Atlantans called it "Atlanta's Berlin Wall" ("Atlanta's 'Berlin Wall'"). Attorney Donald Hollowell took the city to court, and on March 1, 1963, Judge George P. Whitman ruled the barricades unconstitutional; the mayor had them torn down within minutes ("Civil Rights Giant"; "Atlanta's 'Berlin Wall'"). The wall stood for less than three months. Historian Ronald Bayor writes that Allen allowed it as part of an effort to keep the neighborhood white ("Ivan Allen Jr. Biography: Peyton Road Wall").

## The Civil Rights Act Tested in Atlanta (1964)

Sources:
1. "Pickrick Showdown." *The Pickrick Project*, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://sites.gatech.edu/pickrickproject/pickrick-showdown.
2. "Atlanta Restaurant Suit to Test New Rights Act." NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, press release, 10 July 1964, https://ldfrecollection.org/archives/archives-search/archives-item/fa5d67ad-3c25-4068-8e91-55fad85793ae/atlanta-restaurant-suit-to-test-new-rights-act.
3. "Heart of Atlanta Motel Incident." *The Pickrick Project*, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://sites.gatech.edu/pickrickproject/heart-of-atlanta-motel-incident.
4. "Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States 1964." Encyclopedia.com, https://encyclopedia.com/law/legal-and-political-magazines/heart-atlanta-motel-v-united-states-1964.
5. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 170.

South-View Residents Involved
* T.M. Alexander Sr. – Appointed by President Lyndon Johnson to a national citizens' committee to help put the Civil Rights Act of 1964 into practice (pg. 170).

Historical Notes
* President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 on July 2, 1964. It barred businesses open to the public from discriminating by race. (Source 1, timeline)
* On July 3, three ministry students at the Interdenominational Theological Center, George Willis, Albert Dunn, and Woodrow T. Lewis, drove to Lester Maddox's Pickrick restaurant to be served under the new law. Maddox met them with a pistol and called on his customers to grab ax handles he kept in a box by the door, and the three were forced to leave. (Sources 1, 2)
* On July 4, Rev. Charles Wells and Albert Sampson were refused rooms at Moreton Rolleston's Heart of Atlanta Motel. (Sources 1, 3)
* The NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund sued Maddox on behalf of the three students. (Source 2)
* Rolleston sued to overturn the law. On December 14, 1964, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the public accommodations section of the Civil Rights Act in *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States*. (Source 4)

Narrative for website

On July 2, 1964, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act, which barred businesses open to the public from turning people away because of their race ("Pickrick Showdown"). The next day, three Black ministry students at Atlanta's Interdenominational Theological Center, George Willis, Albert Dunn, and Woodrow T. Lewis, drove to Lester Maddox's Pickrick restaurant to be served. Maddox met them with a pistol and called on his customers to grab the ax handles he kept by the door, and the three were driven off ("Pickrick Showdown"; "Atlanta Restaurant Suit to Test New Rights Act"). On July 4, Moreton Rolleston's Heart of Atlanta Motel turned away two more Black ministers ("Heart of Atlanta Motel Incident"). The NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund sued Maddox ("Atlanta Restaurant Suit"), and Rolleston sued to overturn the law. On December 14, 1964, a unanimous Supreme Court upheld the law in *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* ("Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States 1964"). T.M. Alexander Sr., an Atlanta insurance leader whom President Johnson appointed to a national citizens' committee to help put the new law into practice, rests at South-View (pg. 170).

## Grady Hospital Desegregated (1962 – 1965)

Sources:
1. Lovasik, Brendan P., et al. "'The Living Monument': The Desegregation of Grady Memorial Hospital and the Changing South." *The American Surgeon*, vol. 86, no. 3, 2020, pp. 213–19.
2. "Timeline of Major Events." *Grady Hospital Exhibit*, Georgia State University Library, https://exhibits.library.gsu.edu/grady-exhibit/timeline-of-major-events/.
3. "Grady Health System." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/science-medicine/grady-health-system/.
4. "Roy Charles Bell, Pioneering Dentist and Civil Rights Leader." *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution*, https://ajc.com/news/local/roy-charles-bell-pioneering-dentist-and-civil-rights-leader/QJGlfs0QHKcbSa1nIQyoVN.
5. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 171, 174, 257–58.

South-View Residents Involved
* Dr. Asa G. Yancey Sr. – In 1958, the first Black doctor at Grady; in 1972, its medical director (pg. 257–58).
* Eula Benning – A Grady-trained nurse who founded the Atlanta School for Practical Nurse Education (pg. 174).
* Ludie Clay Andrews – Organized Grady's training school for Black nurses in 1914; the Black and white schools were combined in 1965 (pg. 171).

Historical Notes
* Grady Memorial Hospital opened in 1892 in the heart of Atlanta's Black community. Its separate and unequal facilities for Black and white patients led Atlantans to call it "the Gradies." (Sources 1, 3)
* The "New Grady," which opened in 1958, still separated patients by race, wing by wing. (Source 1)
* In 1962, Atlanta dentist Dr. Roy C. Bell sued in federal court. The plaintiffs in *Bell v. Fulton-DeKalb Hospital Authority* included Bell and seven other Black medical professionals, applicants turned away from the Grady School of Nursing, and three Black citizens denied emergency treatment. (Sources 2, 4)
* On June 1, 1965, a memo from hospital superintendent Bill Pinkston put every part of the hospital on a non-racial basis. (Source 1)
* Grady's nursing schools for Black and white students were combined in 1965. (pg. 171)

Narrative for website

Grady Memorial Hospital opened in 1892 in the heart of Black Atlanta, but for more than seventy years it kept Black and white patients in separate and unequal facilities, so that Atlantans called it "the Gradies" (Lovasik et al.; "Grady Health System"). Even the "New Grady" that opened in 1958 split patients by race, wing by wing (Lovasik et al.). In 1962, Atlanta dentist Dr. Roy C. Bell sued in federal court, joined by seven other Black medical professionals, applicants turned away from Grady's nursing school, and three Black citizens who had been denied emergency treatment ("Timeline of Major Events"; "Roy Charles Bell"). On June 1, 1965, a quiet memo from superintendent Bill Pinkston announced, "All phases of the hospital are on a non-racial basis, effective today" (Lovasik et al.). That same year Grady's two nursing schools were combined, ending the separate school for Black nurses that Ludie Clay Andrews had organized in 1914 (pg. 171). Andrews rests at South-View, as do Eula Benning, a Grady-trained nurse, and Dr. Asa G. Yancey Sr., who in 1958 became the first Black doctor at Grady and in 1972 its medical director (pg. 171, 174, 257–58).

## Summerhill Uprising (1966)

Sources:
1. "The Summerhill Riot or the Atlanta Rebellion?: Uprising and Inequity in the 'City Too Busy To Hate.'" Ivan Allen Digital Archive, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://ivanallen.iac.gatech.edu/mayoral-records/traditional/exhibits/show/summerhill-riots.
2. Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. "The Truth." Flyer, Sept. 1966. Ivan Allen Digital Archive, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://ivanallen.iac.gatech.edu/mayoral-records/traditional/items/show/8535.
3. Ivan Allen Jr. Digital Collection, artifact record 373 (Summerhill and Stokely Carmichael), Georgia Institute of Technology, https://ivanallen.iac.gatech.edu/artifacts/items/show/373.
4. "Summerhill Riot." Herbert T. Jenkins Photographs, Atlanta History Center, via Civil Rights Digital Library, 6 Sept. 1966, https://crdl.usg.edu/record/geh_jenkins_17.
5. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 203, 238.

South-View Residents Involved
* Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson – Grew up in Summerhill (pg. 238).
* Charles Walter "Pete" Greenlea – Co-founded and led Summerhill Neighborhood Inc., a community development corporation (pg. 203).

Historical Notes
* On September 6, 1966, Atlanta police shot Harold Prather, a 25-year-old Black man, in Summerhill as they tried to arrest him. (Sources 1, 2)
* Hundreds of residents gathered. Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. climbed onto a police car to urge calm, was heckled, and was knocked off the car. (Sources 3, 4)
* Allen blamed the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). Police raided SNCC's office, and its chairman, Stokely Carmichael, was convicted of inciting to riot. (Sources 1, 3)
* SNCC's flyer "The Truth" said no SNCC members were present when the crowd first gathered outside Prather's home. (Source 2)
* Allen called the events a riot in his memoir; the Georgia Tech archive notes it is also known as the Atlanta Rebellion, and its records describe a demonstration against police brutality. (Sources 1, 3)

Narrative for website

On September 6, 1966, Atlanta police shot Harold Prather, a 25-year-old Black man, as they tried to arrest him in the Summerhill neighborhood ("The Summerhill Riot or the Atlanta Rebellion?"; "The Truth"). Hundreds of residents gathered to protest. Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. came to Summerhill and climbed onto a police car to urge the crowd to go home, but he was heckled and knocked off the car (Ivan Allen Digital Collection). Allen blamed the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC); police raided SNCC's office, and its chairman, Stokely Carmichael, was convicted of inciting a riot (Ivan Allen Digital Collection). SNCC said none of its members were there when the crowd first gathered ("The Truth"). Allen called it a riot. Many Black Atlantans saw an uprising against police violence and the deep inequity of the "City Too Busy to Hate," and it is also remembered as the Atlanta Rebellion ("The Summerhill Riot or the Atlanta Rebellion?"). Ruby Doris Smith-Robinson, a SNCC leader, grew up in Summerhill, and Charles Walter "Pete" Greenlea co-founded Summerhill Neighborhood Inc., a community development corporation; both rest at South-View (pg. 203, 238).

## People

* **Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968)** was born in Atlanta on January 15, 1929, the son of Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. and Alberta Williams King, and grew up in Ebenezer Baptist Church, where his grandfather and then his father were pastor ("Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical"; pg. 220, 254). He graduated from Morehouse College in 1948 and earned his doctorate at Boston University in 1955 ("Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical"). As a young pastor in Montgomery, Alabama, he led the bus boycott that began in December 1955, and in 1957 he became the first president of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference ("Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical"; "Martin Luther King Jr. Fast Facts"). In 1960 he came home to Atlanta as co-pastor of Ebenezer with his father, and that October he was arrested with students at the Rich's sit-in ("Martin Luther King Jr. Fast Facts"; "Atlanta Sit-ins"). In 1964, at thirty-five, he received the Nobel Peace Prize ("Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical"). On April 4, 1968, he was assassinated in Memphis, Tennessee, where he had gone to support striking sanitation workers ("Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical"). Five days later he was laid to rest at South-View, and in January 1970 his body was moved to a tomb near Ebenezer, now part of the King Center (pg. 114–15). His epitaph reads, "Free at last, Free at last, Thank God Almighty, I'm Free at last" (pg. 165).
  * Sources: "Martin Luther King Jr. – Biographical," NobelPrize.org; "Martin Luther King Jr. Fast Facts," CNN; Hatfield, "Atlanta Sit-ins," *New Georgia Encyclopedia*; Henderson, pp. 114–15, 165, 220, 254.
  * Sheet: Rests at South-View = "No, King Center (first laid to rest at South-View, 1968–1970)".

* **Grace Towns Hamilton (1907–1992)**, the daughter of Atlanta University professor George A. Towns and Nellie McNair Towns, graduated from Atlanta University, earned a master's degree at Ohio State University, and taught psychology at Clark College, Atlanta University, and LeMoyne Junior College in Memphis (pg. 204–5, 245). In 1943 she became the first woman to serve as executive director of the Atlanta Urban League (pg. 204). In a June 1965 special election, she and seven Black men won seats in the Georgia House of Representatives, the first Black members of the House since Reconstruction, and she became the first Black woman in the Georgia General Assembly (Spritzer and Bergmark). She served in the House until 1985 and was a principal architect of the 1973 Atlanta city charter, which brought Black Atlantans onto the City Council in proportion to their share of the population (Spritzer and Bergmark; pg. 205). She later advised the U.S. Civil Rights Commission, and in 2006 she was named a Georgia Woman of Achievement (pg. 205). She rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 204–5, 245; Spritzer, Lorraine Nelson, and Jean B. Bergmark. "Grace Towns Hamilton (1907–1992)." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*.

* **Willard L. Strickland (1922–1989)** was born in Atlanta on August 19, 1922, attended Morehouse College, and served in the Army during World War II (pg. 243; "Willard L. Strickland Papers"). In 1948 he was one of the eight Black men who desegregated the Atlanta Police Department (pg. 242). The eight used the Butler Street YMCA as their precinct, could not wear their uniforms to and from work, patrolled Black neighborhoods on foot, and were not allowed to drive squad cars or arrest white people (pg. 242). Strickland left the Atlanta force in 1961, and in 1964 he joined the Decatur Police Department and helped integrate it ("Willard L. Strickland Papers"; pg. 243). He later worked twenty-two years for the U.S. Postal Service in Manhattan, and he received the Atlanta NAACP Honor Award and the Postal Service's Outstanding Service Award (pg. 243). He died on November 1, 1989, and rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 242–43; "Willard L. Strickland Papers," Auburn Avenue Research Library, finding aid.

* **Quentin V. Williamson Sr. (1918–1985)** was born in Atlanta on December 25, 1918, graduated from Morehouse College, and built one of the largest Black-owned real estate firms in the South (pg. 254–55; "Q. V. Williamson and Morris Finley"). Shut out of the white realtors' association, Black real estate agents in Atlanta formed the Empire Real Estate Board in 1939; Williamson served as its president and as president and chairman of the National Association of Real Estate Brokers (pg. 255, 275). In 1965, Atlanta voters elected him to the Board of Aldermen, now the City Council, making him its first Black member since Reconstruction (pg. 254; "1965 Atlanta Board of Aldermen Election"). His leadership on the council, in the Atlanta Negro Voters League, and in other civic groups helped open public and private housing in Atlanta to Black families (pg. 255). "A free man's dream is to own his own home, in the place of his choice," he said (pg. 255). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 254–55, 275; "Q. V. Williamson and Morris Finley," Atlanta History Center, via Digital Library of Georgia, https://dlg.usg.edu/record/geh_byd_939; "1965 Atlanta Board of Aldermen Election," Wikipedia.

* **Rheubin Marcellus Taylor (d. 1991)** taught science and math in the Atlanta Public Schools and served as president of the Georgia Teachers and Education Association, the state's association of Black teachers (pg. 218, 243). At a time when Atlanta paid Black teachers less than white teachers, he and the Atlanta NAACP sued the board of education for equal pay, and NAACP lawyer Constance Baker Motley, later a federal judge, represented the teachers (pg. 243; *Davis v. Cook*). The school system then fired him, saying the reason was not the lawsuit but an absence without leave while he attended his graduate school reunion; the board overturned the firing on appeal but moved him to an elementary school, and he resigned soon after (pg. 243–44). He went on to work seventeen years as a conciliations supervisor for the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (pg. 244). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 218, 243–44; *Davis v. Cook*, 80 F. Supp. 443 (N.D. Ga. 1948) (pay gap between Black and white Atlanta teachers).

* **Roscoe Edwin Thomas Jr. (d. 1996)** graduated from Morehouse College and from the University of Michigan Law School in 1947 (pg. 244). In 1951, Dr. Hamilton M. Holmes, his sons Alfred "Tup" and Oliver Holmes, and Charles Bell were turned away from the city-owned Bobby Jones Golf Course because they were Black ("The Legal Case"). In 1953, with only a few years of experience, Thomas served as lead counsel in *Holmes v. Atlanta*, the first desegregation lawsuit tried in the city (pg. 244). The case reached the U.S. Supreme Court, which ruled on November 7, 1955, and that Christmas Eve and Christmas Day Black golfers played Atlanta's public courses for the first time ("About the Exhibit"; pg. 244). Thomas was a founding member and third president of the Gate City Bar Association and served as general counsel for Citizens Trust Bank, Atlanta Life Insurance Company, and Mutual Federal Savings and Loan (pg. 244). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 244; "The Legal Case" and "About the Exhibit," *Holmes v. Atlanta: Changing the Game*, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://sites.gatech.edu/holmesvatlanta/legalcase.

* **Theodore Martin "T.M." Alexander Sr. (1909–2001)** was born in Montgomery, Alabama, on March 7, 1909, graduated from Morehouse College, and founded Alexander & Company, one of the nation's most successful Black-owned insurance agencies, whose clients included Coca-Cola, the City of Atlanta, and Fulton County (pg. 170; "T.M. Alexander, Sr. Papers"). During the Montgomery Bus Boycott, when insurance companies canceled coverage on the church station wagons that carried boycotters to work, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. turned to Alexander, who arranged insurance through Lloyd's of London so the carpools could keep running (pg. 170; "Clarence L. Jordan"). A friend of King, he served as president of the Atlanta branch of the NAACP and of the Empire Real Estate Board (pg. 170). After the Civil Rights Act of 1964, President Lyndon Johnson appointed him to a national citizens' committee to help put the new law into practice (pg. 170). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 170; "T.M. Alexander, Sr. Papers," Auburn Avenue Research Library, finding aid, https://aafa.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/6; "Clarence L. Jordan," The Martin Luther King, Jr., Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/clarence-l-jordan.

* **Dr. Frederick Earl McLendon (d. 1981) and Bennie Bell McLendon (d. 1995).** Dr. McLendon, a native of Washington, Georgia, and a graduate of Meharry Medical College, opened the McLendon Medical Clinic for Black Atlantans in 1946; by the time the hospital closed in 1981, more than fifty Black and four hundred white doctors had trained there (pg. 227). *Jet* magazine called him a "militant medic" (pg. 227). As president of the Atlanta Medical Association, the city's association of Black doctors, he wired Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. to offer doctors and medical aid to victims of police brutality in Selma, joined other doctors in demonstrations for equal pay for African Americans, and was arrested with other doctors at a medical conference when they tried to eat lunch in the segregated cafeteria of Atlanta's Biltmore Hotel (pg. 227, 231). Bennie Bell McLendon, a Fisk University graduate whom her husband called the "brain power" behind the hospital, served as its secretary for thirty-five years and led the women's auxiliaries of the Atlanta Medical Association, the Georgia State Medical Association, and the National Medical Association (pg. 228). She was co-founder and first president of the Atlanta chapter of The Links, and with Dorothy Alexander she led the protest to desegregate the Metropolitan Opera's annual Atlanta performances at the Fox Theatre (pg. 170, 228). Both rest at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 170, 227–28, 231.

* **Dr. Asa Greenwood Yancey Sr. (1916–2013)** was born in Atlanta on August 19, 1916, the son of mail carrier Arthur H. Yancey and Daisy Sherard Yancey, who led the Neighborhood Union from 1950 until it disbanded in 1962 (pg. 257; "A Long and Distinguished Career"). He graduated from Morehouse College and the University of Michigan Medical School, studied under Dr. Charles R. Drew at Freedmen's Hospital in Washington, D.C., and spent ten years as chief of surgery at the Tuskegee Veterans Hospital (pg. 257). In 1958 he came home to Grady Memorial Hospital as chief of surgery at its Hughes Spalding Pavilion, becoming the first Black doctor at Grady, and he became the first Black faculty member of Emory University's medical school (pg. 257). At Emory he started the first accredited surgical training program for Black doctors in Georgia, and in 1972 he became medical director of Grady and an associate dean of Emory's medical school (pg. 257–58). He also served on the Atlanta Board of Education (pg. 258). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 257–58; "A Long and Distinguished Career," Department of Surgery, Emory University School of Medicine, https://med.emory.edu/departments/surgery/education/general-surgery-residency/asa-yancey-bio.html; "Asa G. and Carolyn D. Yancey Papers," Atlanta History Center, finding aid, https://ahc.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/3187.

* **Eula Benning (d. 1964)** graduated from the Grady School of Nursing and earned a bachelor of science in nursing education at New York University (pg. 174). She supervised public health nurses for Atlanta and Fulton County and served on the executive committee of the National Nursing Association (pg. 174). She worked for the education and professional recognition of Black nurses: in 1963 she founded the Atlanta School for Practical Nurse Education, and as the last president of Georgia's association of Black nurses she helped bring it into the state's association of white nurses (pg. 174). At her funeral in 1964, practical nurses who had graduated from her school only two weeks before served as flower ladies (pg. 174). The Atlanta chapter of the Grady Nurses Conclave, the Eula Benning Club, is named in her honor (pg. 174). She rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 174.

* **John Henry Calhoun Jr. (1899–1988)** earned his college degree from Morehouse College at thirty-eight and a master's degree from Atlanta University at sixty-nine (pg. 185). He co-founded the Atlanta Negro Voters League, chaired the Atlanta NAACP's Legal Redress Committee, and in 1956 became president of the Atlanta branch of the NAACP (pg. 185; "John H. Calhoun, Jr. Papers"). When a court ordered him to turn over the branch's records, he refused and went to jail for contempt (pg. 185; "John H. Calhoun, Jr. Papers"). During the sit-ins he worked with the students to desegregate Atlanta's lunch counters and stores ("John H. Calhoun, Jr. Papers"). In 1973, at seventy-four, he was elected to the Atlanta City Council, where he introduced the resolution that created the city's Neighborhood Planning Units, which give residents a voice in city planning (pg. 185; "John H. Calhoun, Jr. Papers"). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 185; "John H. Calhoun, Jr. Papers," Robert W. Woodruff Library, Atlanta University Center, finding aid, https://findingaids.auctr.edu/repositories/2/resources/91.

* **Virgil Wendell Hodges (d. 1985)** was a sociology professor and department chair at Clark College, later dean of Albany State College, and city editor of the *Atlanta Daily World* (pg. 212). In the 1940s he chaired the administrative board of the Atlanta chapter of the NAACP, and when a judge ordered him to turn over a list of the chapter's members, he refused (pg. 212). Decades later, in the 1980s, he was the most prolific membership chairman in the chapter's history (pg. 213). His wife, Ruth Hall Hodges, chaired the art department at Morris Brown College and was the first Black woman in Atlanta to own a photography studio (pg. 213). He rests at South-View.
  * Sources: Henderson, pp. 212–13.

* **Dr. Horace Mann Bond (1904–1972)**, a historian and educator, was born in Nashville, Tennessee, on November 8, 1904 (pg. 177; "Horace Mann Bond"). He was the first president of Fort Valley State College, from 1939 to 1945, and the first Black president of Lincoln University, his alma mater, from 1945 to 1957 (pg. 177; "Horace Mann Bond"). With historians C. Vann Woodward and John Hope Franklin, he did research for the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund in *Brown v. Board of Education* (pg. 177). From 1957 until shortly before his death he was dean of the school of education at Atlanta University (pg. 177). His wife, Julia Washington Bond, earned her master's degree in library science at fifty-six and worked as a librarian at Atlanta University and the Robert W. Woodruff Library until she retired at ninety-two, and their son, Julian Bond, helped organize SNCC (pg. 177). He died in Atlanta on December 21, 1972, and rests at South-View (pg. 177).
  * Sources: Henderson, p. 177; "Horace Mann Bond." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/education/horace-mann-bond-1904-1972/.

## Discrepancies found (not for the cards)

1. **Grace Towns Hamilton's seat.** Henderson (pg. 204–5) says she served in the Georgia *Senate* until 1984 and that no African American had served in the legislature since Reconstruction. The *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, the AUC finding aid and her papers say she served in the Georgia *House* from 1965 to 1985, and NGE says the eight were the first Black members of the *lower house* since Reconstruction (Leroy Johnson had been elected to the state Senate in 1962). The card follows NGE.
2. **John H. Calhoun Jr.'s records.** Henderson (pg. 185) says he refused to hand over the branch's *financial* records; the AUC finding aid says the *membership list*. The card says "the branch's records."
3. **Rheubin Taylor's lawsuit.** Henderson (pg. 243) says that in 1948 a federal court ordered Georgia's public schools to equalize teacher pay. The 1948 Atlanta case appears to be *Davis v. Cook* (N.D. Ga., Sept. 28, 1948), brought by a Black Atlanta teacher named Davis for all Black teachers and principals; Taylor is not named in the opinion we saw, and we did not confirm how the case ended on appeal. The card uses the case only for the pay gap, leaves out the 1948 order, and keeps Henderson's general wording ("he and the Atlanta NAACP sued").
4. **Virgil Hodges and the NAACP list.** Only Henderson (pg. 212) has the 1940s refusal. It is similar to Calhoun's 1956 jailing. Worth asking Ms. Hemphill whether this is the same episode.
5. **Peyton Road dates.** Sources give December 17 or 18, 1962, and March 1 or March 11, 1963. The card uses the December 17 aldermen vote and March 1 ruling (*Atlanta* magazine, *Atlanta Inquirer*, and Mayor Allen's March 4, 1963 letters thanking people for their views on "Judge Whitman's decision").
6. **Harold Prather.** One archive description says police killed him; another says he was wounded. The card says only that police shot him.
7. **Grady lawsuit.** A 2026 Georgia House resolution and an AUC exhibit call *Bell v. Fulton-DeKalb Hospital Authority* a Supreme Court ruling; the AJC and the GSU exhibit describe a federal case. The card says "sued in federal court."
8. **Asa Yancey.** Sources give 1958 or 1964 as the year he joined Emory's faculty, and March 9 or 10, 2013, as his date of death. The card gives neither.
9. **T.M. Alexander's committee.** Henderson calls it the "National Citizens Committee for Human Relations"; the official name could not be confirmed, so the card says "a national citizens' committee."
10. **SCLC naming.** Sources differ on whether the name SCLC was adopted in February or August 1957. The card says "later that year."
